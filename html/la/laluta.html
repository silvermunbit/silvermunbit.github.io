1 Introduction

This is a personal constructed language. Its name is läl'üta lalűta, which comes directly from the word meaning language. It does not attempt to be naturalistic, it is not set in any fictional world, nor does it have a fictional group of speakers. It is free of loan words and makes no reference to names of countries, people, brands, culture-specific ideas, etc. existing in the real world. Its purpose is self-expression, not creating or translating specialized or technical texts.

As this is a personal language, its "flavor," that is idiomatic expressions, register, and anything that in a natural language would be culture-guided, is based on my own thoughts, experiences, and worldviews. The language is not inspired by any particular language, natural or fictional, though it draws bits and pieces from many.

Although the language is not naturalistic, it features elements such as non-concatenation, proto-forms, grammaticalization, suppletion, and the occasional semantic shift, in order to make it more interesting. The language is exclusively head-initial, VSO and low-context, though it encourages the use of figurative and metaphoric expressions.

Due to the complexity of the language and the length of this document, mistakes in spelling, grammar and syntax in the presented examples are bound to be present. This is why I provide thorough explanations of the discussed grammatical, morphological, syntactical, etc. constructions and strategies. With the use of these descriptions, potential mistakes can be corrected.

2 Phonology


2.1 Vowels

Vowels are divided into front and back vowels. This influences the realization of preceding consonants and the forms of some adjacent morphological units. Non-low vowels can be realized as glides when found next to other vowels (see 2.5 Vowel hiatus). Note that ä and a have the same pronunciation, but the former is front while the latter is back.

Front ä e i ü a e i ü /a ɛ i y/
Back a o u a o u /a ʌ ɯ/
Glides i/e u/o ü y~i w~u ẅ~ü /j ɰ ɥ/

Glides /j ɰ ɥ/are transcribed as y w ẅ:

  • - word-initially, e.g. iusca yusca, lob wov, üora ẅona
  • - between vowels, e.g. caio cayo, olo owo, poüeliuc poẅeluc
  • - after high vowels, e.g. curpil cuypiw, bilp viwp, tiü chiẅ
  • - throughout this document: in isolation except after consonants, e.g. lsp wsp, rp' ypi

They are transcribed as i u ü otherwise, e.g. bäd viad, lein lein, müi müi.

2.3 Phonetic notes

◆ Obstruents

Onset voiceless stops are aspirated, especially when stressed, e.g. pop pop [pʰʌp]. Aspiration before glides results in their devoicing, e.g. cüä qua [kɰ̊a].

/ɡ/ is realized as [ŋ] between vowels and glides, e.g. agaem agaim [äŋäjm], cagoeli cagueli [kʰäŋɰɛli].

/c/ and /ɟ/ are realized as [t͡ɕ] and [d͡ʑ], e.g. iti ichi [it͡ɕi], sodia soja [sʌd͡ʑa]. /c/ is realized as [c] only after the non-sibilant [ç], e.g. äsgir aschir [äçciɹ].

Word-final /t/ can be flapped [ɾ] if followed by a vowel, e.g. rot olo not owo [nʌɾ ʌɰʌ].

/s/ is slightly palatalized. Alternatively, it can be a hypersibilant or a lisped s.

◆ Sonorants

/ɰ̃/ is not technically a separate phoneme, but a neutralization of /m/ and /n/ in coda position when directly preceded by a vowel. However, I have decided to treat it as a phoneme, because leaving it as /m/ and /n/ would suggest different realizations, and picking one over the other begs the question why should one be chosen over the other. /ɰ̃/ lowers preceding high vowels to mid vowels, meaning only the following three combinations are possible.

  • - an /aɰ̃/ for äN aN. For example, cän chan, bamt vant.
  • - en /ɛɰ̃/ for eN iN üN. For example, bems viens, min mien, ümpüi empüi.
  • - on /ʌɰ̃/ for oN uN. For example, momc monc, sun son.

/ɰ̃/ has three allophones.

  • - [ɥ̃] before /pʲ vʲ/, e.g. pumpeom pompion [pʌɥ̃pʲʌɰ̃], sonbis sonvis [sʌɥ̃vʲis].
  • - [j̃] before /c ɟ ç/, e.g. nantee nanchi [näj̃t͡ɕi], ondi onji [ʌj̃d͡ʑi].
  • - [ɰ̃] elswhere, e.g. peom pion [pʲʌɰ̃], ponca ponca [pʰʌɰ̃kä].

Coda /m/ and /n/ assimilate to following obstruents. They are romanized as m before p, and n otherwise.

  • - [m] before /p/, e.g. poinp poimp [pʰʌjmp].
  • - [mʲ] before /pʲ/, e.g. poinpiugu poimpiugu [pʰʌjmʲpʲɯŋɯ].
  • - [ɱ] before /v/, e.g. eümbu eünvu [ɛɥɱvɯ].
  • - [ɱʲ] before /vʲ/, e.g. sainbee sainvi [säjɱʲvʲi].
  • - [n] before /t d s/, e.g. coünt coünt [kʰʌɥnt], adainst adainst [ädäjnst].
  • - [ɲ] before /c ɟ ç/, e.g. coünteom coünchon [kʰʌɥɲt͡ɕʌɰ̃].
  • - [ŋ] before /k ɡ/, e.g. soingoält soinguaut [sʌjŋɡɰäɰt].

Coda /ɹ/ changes the pronunciation of some preceding vowels.

  • - ar /aɹ/ for är ar. For example, lilärn lilarn, parc parc.
  • - ir /iɹ/ for ir. For example, gir jir.
  • - ür /ɥɚ/ for ür. For example, nür nür, mürp mürp.
  • - ur /ɚ/ for er or ur. For example, ter chur, tors turs.

When the rhotic follows a glide, it is realized as /ɚ/, e.g. soir soir /sʌjɚ/. The additional rhotic vowel is purely epenthetic and does not count as a separate syllable nucleus for the purposes of counting word stress, cf. carer cayur /kaˈjɚ/ and cair cair /ˈkajɚ/.

Non-epenthetic /jɚ ɥɚ ɰɚ/ are romanized as yur yür wur, e.g. alur awur, nütarür nütayür.

◆ Geminates

Geminates are always realized as geminates, never as doubled consonants, e.g. äbba appa [apːa], not [*ap.pa].

Geminates are romanized by doubling the consonant letter, with a few exceptions:

  • - geminate ch is romanized as tch. For example, ecci etchi.
  • - geminate q is romanized as cq. For example, äbcuä acqua.
  • - geminate glides y w ẅ are romanized as iy uw üẅ before non-high vowels. For example, piireli paiyeli, iuscaulo yuscauwo.
  • - geminate glides y w ẅ are romanized as yy ww ẅẅ before high vowels. For example, currio cuyyo.

For the purposes of word stress counting, syllables preceding a geminate are closed.

◆ Vowels

/a/ has three allophones.

  • - [æ] before /ɹ/, e.g. sar sar [sæɹ]
  • - [a] after palatal consonants /pʲ vʲ mʲ c ɟ ɲ l j ɥ/, e.g. pälsc piausc [pʲaɰsk], diiis jais [d͡ʑajs], räbee yavi [javi].
  • - [ä] otherwise, e.g. ada ada [ädä], ciilm caim [kʰäjm], lag wag [ɰäɡ].

Other vowels have the following realizations.

  • - /ɛ/ is the same as English dress in GenAm, around [ɛ], e.g. les les [lɛs]
  • - /i/ is fully close and front, around [i], e.g. lis lis [lis]
  • - /y/ is also fully close and front, around [y], e.g. l'üs lüs [lys]
  • - /ʌ/ is similar to French /ɔ/ but without lip rounding, quite fronted, around [ʌ̟~ɐ], e.g. leos los [lʌ̟s~lɐs]
  • - /ɯ/ is similar to Japanese u or Korean eu, somewhat fronted and unrounded, around [ɯ̟~ɨ], e.g. l'iusca lusca [lɯ̟skä~lɨskä]. It is slightly rhotacized before a pause, e.g. liu lu [lɯ̟˞~lɨ˞].

High vowels /i y ɯ/ can occur directly after other vowels. To prevent hiatus, epenthetic semivowels /j ɥ ɰ/ are inserted between the two. These sequences are romanized yi yü wu, e.g. marriga mayiga /majiɡa/, uloo uwu /ɯɰɯ/, tarü tayü /taɥy/.

◆ Glides

Two glides cannot cooccur adjacently within the same syllable. The latter is elided, e.g. doils /*dʌjɰs/ → /dʌjs/ dois.

Outside of epenthesis, /j/ is not pronounced when adjacent to /i y/, and /ɰ ɥ/ are not pronounced when adjacent to /ɯ y/, e.g. rütnü /*jyjɲy/ → /yɲy/ ünü, l'ugu /*ɰɯɡɯ/ → /ɯɡɯ/ ugu. This means onset r before /i j y ɥ/ and l before /ɯ ɰ/ are effectively silent, e.g. rüd /*jyd/ → /yd/ üd, rio /*jjʌ/ → /jʌ/ yo.

The only other environment in which /j/ can occur next to /i y/, and /ɰ ɥ/ can occur next to /ɯ y/, is when the vowel is first and in a separate syllable, e.g. biio viyo /vi.jʌ/, muloä muwa /mɯ.ɰa/.

When a syllable ending in a glide is followed by hiatus, the following realizations apply.

Examples include:

  • - toulools /*tʌɰ.ɯs/ → /tʌ.ɰɯs/ towus.
  • - cura tiülura /*kɯna ciɥ.ɯna/ → /kɯna ci.ɰɯna/ cuna chiwúna
  • - isrig täorig /*içiɡ caɰ.iɡ/ → /içiɡ ca.ɰiɡ/ ihig chawig
  • - irlü /*iɰ.ɥy/ → /iɥy/ iyü
◆ Miscellaneous

Word boundaries typically block palatalization, excluding certain set phrases or collocations; this also affects romanization, e.g. läl üta lal üta.

In fast speech, palatalization can spread between word boundaries, e.g. coünst mins mibis nä coünst miens mivis nia [kʰʌɥçc mʲɛj̃ç mʲivʲiç ɲa].

2.4 Stress and phonotactics

Word stress is final if the syllable is closed, otherwise penultimate if the syllable is closed, otherwise antepenultimate. Every other preceding syllable receives secondary stress. In case of exceptions, stress may be marked with an acute in the romanization. The exceptions to the stress rule include:

  • - compound SDOAUX, which are always stressed on the final syllable, e.g. arärä ayayá
  • - second person verb forms ending in -ciu, which are always stressed on the penult, e.g. eliciu elíchu
  • - adjectives and nouns involved in NSWEO agreement, e.g. bee olee vi olí, lica liboa licá livuá
  • - certain short compounds, where stress can be counted as if two words were used, e.g. läl'üta lalűta (from läl üta lal üta)
  • - certain clipped compounds, e.g. cada cadá (from ca darn ca darn)

The maximum syllable shape is CVC, but:

  • - word-initially sP is allowed
  • - word-medially WsP is allowed
  • - word-finally sP WP Ws WsP LN are allowed

Word-initially, clusters soften in their entirety before front vowels, meaning the initial s changes to /ç/, although no change is made to the romanization, e.g. spee spi, stic schic, scä scha. Word-medially, clusters can also be hard or soft (see 2.6 Consonant clusters). Word-finally, clusters are always hard.

Any instances of word-initial or word-final sb sd sg are respelled as sp st sc, e.g. s- + -darc → starc, pag + -s- → pasc.

  • - y is never pronounced as /j/ after consonants, rather it palatalizes the preceding consonant, e.g. gios /*ɟjʌs/ → /ɟʌs/ jos, mion /*mjʌɰ̃/ → /mʲʌɰ̃/ mion.

  • - TYPE 1: glide i ü u followed by double vowel digraph (iee uoo üoo disallowed), e.g. ocnioo ouniu, diii jai.
  • - TYPE 2: glide + vowel (from table above) followed by a glide (higher than preceding vowel), e.g. muao muau, saedoeic saidueic.
  • - TYPE 3: vowel followed by glide + vowel (from table above, except for eä ea oä oa after non-low vowels and eo oe after all vowels), e.g. eia eya, puio puyo. These allow for additional vowels in their reduplicated forms, e.g. biio → biioio viyoyo.

2.7 Umlaut

Umlaut involves the mutation of a vowel or a vowel sequence when it is followed by an appended front high vowel. This can occur when an inflectional or derivational affix is attached to a word, and in SDOAUX (see 4.4 Clause-final auxiliaries). It does not apply between morphemes in compounds or across word boundaries, so articles, demonstratives and adjectives do not trigger umlaut.

3 Orthography


3.1 Overview

The script is a semi-syllabary written vertically, with columns read from left to right. The principal feature of the script is a vertical line going through the middle of each column. To the left of the line, the onset and nucleus are encoded. To the right, the coda and/or the voicing of the onset are encoded. In addition, several characters exist for encoding non-CVC syllables.

The script also features four logographs encoding certain grammatical morphology, and a simple system of punctuation.

3.2 Syllabic characters

(Note that the word syllable used across the following sections refers to orthographic syllables, rather than phonological ones.)

◆ CVC characters

The size of a standard character is 20x20u (units). In each word, a vertical line runs through the middle. To the left of the line, syllabic glyphs representing the onset and nucleus of each syllable are placed; this includes CV and V glyphs. To the right of the line, glyphs encoding codas are placed if necessary. All syllabic glyphs can be found in Appendix 2: Syllabary.

a cü no
at cün nod

Each glyph occupies a space of 10x17.5u, leaving 2.5u at the bottom to prevent an overlap with the glyph below. The only exceptions are glyphs which transition into the line smoothly at the bottom, such as the following.

ü ci su

The right side of the line can also encode voicing of the onset. There are no separate glyphs for pairs such as ca and ga. Instead, voicing is encoded with a glyph placed to the right of the line, following the voiceless initial glyph. Compare the following:

ca ga te de

Because the coda glyphs and the voicing glyph occupy the same space, each coda has two versions - one which encodes the coda alone, and one which encodes both the coda and the voicing of the onset. Compare the following:

co go cos gos

When a syllable with an empty right side is followed by a V syllable (but not VC), the latter is mirrored and placed to the right of the former, rather than underneath it. Compare the following:

seon seo dau tau

All examples presented thus far have described characters as used word-internally. However, word-initially and word-finally, the vertical line does not extend throughout the entire height of the character, but the excess is cut off. The points at which the characters connect to the line are hereinafter referred to as vertices. The line begins at the highest vertex of the initial character, and ends at the lowest vertex of the final character.

micoso adabois eusat

The only exceptions to this rule are the syllabic glyph pa and the voicing glyph. The vertical line does not connect the two vertices of either glyph. When either of the glyphs is combined with one on the other side of the line, the latter connects to the vertical line, which then merges with the higher vertex of the former, unless it is a B glyph which transitions into the line smoothly at the bottom.

panpapas dagiba
◆ Non-CVC characters

The characters described in the previous sections are sufficient when encoding CVC syllables. For more complex syllables, additional characters are used. Interconsonantal s is encoded with a separate 20x20u character. Word-initial clustering s and word-final clustering consonants are encoded using 30x10u characters, though these characters do not need to occupy the entire available space.

stag dansca mirn inst

Non-CVC characters are also used to encode consonants which are the codas of syllables encompassed in a preceding logograph.

äscet äb'n elit
◆ Monogrammic words

Words contained within the height of a single character (20u) are problematic in that the same character simultaneously begins and ends the word. To resolve this, several strategies are employed. Each glyph is categorized based on the manner in which it connects to the vertical line. It can either connect at the top (T), at the bottom (B), in the middle (M), or throughout the length of the line (W). The glyph pa and the voicing glyph are exceptions and will be described separately. These categories can be found in Appendix 2: Syllabary. Depending on this categorization, different strategies are employed when encoding monogrammic words.

If the word contains a glyph on both sides of the line, and one constituent is T while the other is B, the two are connected by the vertical line between their respective vertices.

rot cän cus

When a glyph transitioning smoothly into the bottom of the line is one of the constituents of a monogrammic word, its form is identical to the one used word-initially. This includes the voicing glyph and the pa glyph.

gir gä bam pam pac bas

In other cases of combining two glyphs, the line starts at the highest vertex of the pair, and extends below the character, ending in a 5x5u left-facing loop.

pic mis ar

When only a left-side glyph is used, in most cases right-facing loops are used. For B glyphs, the line extends up from the vertex and ends in a loop at the top of the character box. For T and M glyphs, the line extends down from the vertex and ends in a loop at the bottom of the character box. For W glyphs, the line connects its highest and lowest vertices. For the pa glyph, the form is identical to the one used word-initially.

ci nü ä mi ti su pa
◆ Syllabification

Syllable breaks avoid coda glyphs followed by vowel glyphs regardless of etymology. For instance, while peom is syllabified as pe-om, peomu is syllabified as pe-o-mu, not pe-om-u. Several compounds break this rule, which is marked in the transliteration with an apostrophe, e.g. läl'üta (syllabified as läl-ü-ta rather than lä-lü-ta). See the examples below.

peom peomu läl'üta

  • - when encoding the words that the logographs etymologically originate from, e.g. eli see, piir differ
  • - when encoding conjunctions and prepositions sharing etymology with logographs, e.g. pagson from, since, dar or

3.4 Punctuation and spacing

There are three basic punctuation marks, each 5x5u. They attach directly to the nearest character. If said character does not connect to the line, punctuation marks are instead placed directly before it (for word-final characters) or after it (for word-initial characters). All non-word-final punctuation marks have a 2.5u vertical line buffer separating them from the following character.

The period () is used at the end of each sentence. If the final character in the sentence contains a B or W glyph, the period is turned upside-down ().

pal. cis. mirn. momc.

The comma () is used at the end of each non-sentence-final clause, after each item used with ä(b)/e(l) and, before dar or, and at the end of adverbial clauses which precede the predicate.

la, mis, darc,

The break () is used for all other purposes. It can be used as a hyphen (transliterated/romanized as -), to separate gerunds from suffixing case marking/prepositions, in which case it is used word-internally. If the final character before the word-internal break does not connect to the line, the break instead connects to the following character and is written without its bottom-right side. There is a single space (5u) separating it from the preceding character, though this space is purely visual and does not count as a word separator.

näl-son coünt-äbee

The break can be used as a colon (transliterated/romanized as :), marking the start of a list or explanation.

micaga: bamt:

Finally, the break can be used as brackets (transliterated/romanized as ( )) in order to isolate a segment of text.

(stag) (mirn)

Words are separated by spaces whose placement is consistent with the transliteration. The space between two words is always 5u. Each column of text is 30u wide. The horizontal space between each column is 2.5u wide.

These ligatures contain a maximum of one full-height character, but may contain 30x10u initial or final glyphs. 3.6 Notes on transliteartion and romanization

Although question marks and exclamation marks are not used in the original orthography, they may be used in questions and exclamations in the transliteration and romanization for clarity.

There is no capitalization in the original orthography, but in the transliteration and romanization, sentences conventionally begin with capital letters. Proper nouns are not capitalized since the language makes no use of proper nouns.

Accent marks are auxiliary in nature, and are used throughout this document and the dictionary for clarity. They can be disregarded, especially in case some characters are unavailable, e.g. läl'üta lalűta → lalüta.

In stylized romanizations, for esthetic or artistic purposes, several other changes can be freely made.

  • - an en on representing a vowel followed by /ɰ̃/ can be respelled as aõ/aũ eõ/eũ oũ. For example, sadan → sadaõ/sadaũ, lent → leõt/leũt, gont → goũt.
  • - au ua ai ia can be respelled as ao oa ae ea. For example, niau → neao, vuala → voala, daip → daep.
  • - consonantal i u can be respelled as y w. For example, piausc → pyawsc, pair → payr.
  • - ẅ can be respelled as ÿ. For example, ẅent → ÿent, jiẅta → jiÿta.

The transliteration and romanization used throughout this document is an updated version created two years after the initial completion of the project. For clarity, I will include all differences between the old and new conventions:

  • - c was transliterated as k
  • - /k/ was consistently romanized as k
  • - preconsonantal /ç/ was romanized as h

4 Overarching structures


4.1 Class

Verbs, adjectives and nouns are divided into one of five classes named after cardinal directions; a system referred to as NSWEO. This is similar to grammatical gender, though gender is typically associated with agreement and intrinsic categorization, while this system involves harmony and semantic associations. Combinations of words within the same class are considered semantically stronger and may be used in idioms and collocations. Adjective endings are changed to rhyme with that of the noun if they have the same class. The only form of class agreement is seen in demonstratives and articles.

There are third person pronouns for each of the five classes, though animate referents are not bound to a single class. The classes rather suggest certain features or traits, so the same referent may switch class depending on the context determined by the judgment of the speaker, e.g. a person seen as good by the speaker may be S, while someone perceived as an introvert may be N. Inanimate referents typically stay within one class, though the class may be changed for metaphoric reasons. Each class has a number of possible endings (* in the table means it includes clusters, e.g. L* = L LP Ls LsP LN).

Class Name Examples of associations Endings
North (N) pil piw cold, dark, bad, calm, quiet, still, weak L*
South (S) rio yo hot, bright, good, lively, loud, move, strong B e o
West (W) abi avi end, old, death, short, hear P ä i
East (E) mon mon start, new, young, life, long, say N* u
Neutral (O) püta püta human, nature s* ü a

There are several grammatical words belonging to the O class despite their endings, including gä this, lä what and pronouns ending in either of the two, as well as all other interrogatives and demonstratives. A number of indefinite pronouns which use the negative article as an enclitic retain their class, ignoring the ending of the article, e.g. cusan no one, someone is O, not E.

Several clipped compounds retain their class despite clipping, for example cusca same person (S because it is a clipped form of cus cago).

◆ North and south

The north class (gata ci spil gata chi spiw) is found on the value axis (rotoüt a spipa notoüt a spipa) along with the south class. North words are low in value and associated with concepts such as badness, darkness, cold and weakness. As such, north is found at the bottom of the compass. Apart from negative concepts, north is also related to silence, serenity, stillness, cleanliness, and intelligence. A person described as north could be perceived as cold or indifferent, but the class can also be ascribed to someone quiet, introverted, logical, or calm and collected.

The south class (gata ci serio gata chi hieyo) is found on the opposite side of the value axis. South is related to concepts opposite of those classified as north - goodness, light, heat, and strength. It is found at the top of the compass. Other south concepts include movement, passion and lust. A south person is typically extroverted, open, passionate, brave, curious, but may be stupid, turbulent, and easily swayed by emotions.

◆ West and east

The west class (gata ci s'abi gata chi savi) is found on the existence axis (rotoüt a s'adabois notoüt a sadavois) along with the east class. West signifies finality, death, shortness and destruction. It is also associated with features such as stoicism and independence. West also conveys concreteness and action. The class is found on the right side of the compass. A west person is perceived as a realist, or someone who prefers acting based on observing the world around them. Those who enjoy conflict or competition can also be described as west.

The east class (gata ci somon gata chi somon) is found on the opposite side of the existence axis and on the left side of the compass. It is associated with genesis, life, longevity and creation. Traits such as kindness, nurturing and care are also east. Since the class is associated with creation, it encompasses abstract concepts and focuses on thought rather than action. East is the class of all artists and those sensitive to beauty, as well as people seen as kind, agreeable and caring.

◆ Neutral

The neutral class (gata ci spüta gata chi spüta) is placed in the middle of the compass and is the balance to the remaining four classes. All neutral concepts are covered by this class. This includes generic words such as nature, type, shape, image, place and sound. Among neutral words are also those which balance out a north/south or west/east pair, such as temperature (heat + cold). The neutral class can be ascribed to any person and is used to remove any emotional association with the referent. As such, it can be used when addressing a group of people, since those are highly unlikely to be homogeneous, or an unknown person, for example in questionnaires. The neutral pronoun is also the pronominal equivalent of indefinite pronouns such as cusan no one, someone and mican nothing, something.

4.2 Number

Nouns, as well as first and second person pronouns inflect for number using an animacy-based system. Nouns are divided into one of five classes, each having a default, "expected" number which is unmarked, and an "unexpected" number which is marked identically across all animacy classes (via reduplication). These classes are independent of NSWEO.

Class Unmarked Marked
1 unique nouns nid (the) sun ninid suns, (a) sun
2 nouns coming in sets püi (a pair of) eyes püibüi (an) eye, (many) eyes
3 other animate nouns cus person cugus people
4 mass nouns nal water nanal (a glass of) water, (a) water
5 inanimate nouns bamt flower(s) babamt (a) flower

Class 1 nouns comprises unique referents, such as nid the sun, bis the world, as well as numeric nouns like saba two, bota thousand. Class 1 nouns are marked when the instance does not refer to a unique referent, e.g. ninid suns, bibis r'än my world.

Class 2 nouns are those which typically occur in a specific number, e.g. days of the week (7), seasons (4), eyes (2), parents (2). Class 2 nouns are unmarked when referred to as a whole set or a general concept, and marked when referring to only one member of the set, or when occurring in any other unexpected number, cf. püi (a pair of) eyes, eyes (in general), püibüi an eye, more than two eyes. Because they are expected to occur in a specific number, class 1 and 2 nouns are collectively referred to as set nouns.

Class 3 nouns are any other animate nouns - people or animals. They are unmarked when singular and marked when plural, e.g. cus person, cugus people.

Class 4 nouns are mass nouns, and are unmarked by default and marked when specifying a unit or a specific instance, e.g. nal water → nanal a glass/bottle/unit of water, stag fire → stadag a fire (a campfire/fire on a match, etc.).

Class 5 nouns are other inanimate nouns. They are ambiguous to number and the marked form is applied only to explicitly specify one of something. For abastract nouns, this means that the base form refers to general concepts and the marked one to specific instances, e.g. saecuic murder → Neo saecuguic ari. There's been a murder., caio love → Äb'et caioio diibi misan usa läläb. Our love will never die. For most other nouns, the marked form is generally only used in a contrastive sense to resolve potential ambiguities, or when a plurality is expected, e.g. paiman cookie(s) → Peobeoms paimaman nänon. I baked a cookie (normally people make more).

4.3 Case

There are three cases - direct, genitive, and dative.

The direct case:

  • - marks subjects of sentences and clauses, e.g. Col milm tarc. A cat is sleeping. (sleep-3P cat 3NUM.)
  • - marks direct objects of sentences and clauses, e.g. Soirn bisnä närä. A cat is sleeping. (sleep-3P cat 1SG-3WUM.)
  • - is used after prepositions governed by the direct case, e.g. Arasc bans col nä. I sit in order to rest. (sit-1P for rest-GER 1SG.)

The genitive case:

  • - marks possession, e.g. lein r'ecci a man's book (book GEN-man)
  • - marks a general relationship between two nouns, e.g. asoir ermilm cat food (food GEN-cat)
  • - marks time or place of origin, e.g. Pa s'ugu pa. He goes from a house. (go-3P GEN-house 3OUM.)
  • - marks a topic, e.g. Sansc somuingoä pa. He writes about history. (write-3P GEN-history 3OUM.)
  • - marks verbs in attributive clauses, e.g. milm scol a sleeping cat (cat GEN-sleep-GER)
  • - marks subjects in intransitive subordinate clauses, e.g. bans pagpeo r'an because a child walks (for walk-GER GEN-child)
  • - marks objects in transitive subordinate clauses, e.g. bans soir bans gor ergil because a bear eats a fish (for eat-GER by bear GEN-fish)
  • - negates copulae, e.g. Lä s'ugu leüt arä. A tree is not a house. (COP-3P GEN-house tree 3WUM.)
  • - is used after prepositions governed by the genitive case, e.g. Soirn rot diisop a nä. I eat with my hands. (eat-1P by GEN-hand DEF.ART 1SG.)

The dative case:

  • - marks indirect objects, e.g. Dobee äbmomc pa. He talks to a woman. (talk-3P DAT-woman 3OUM.)
  • - marks time, e.g. Caült linidresin pa. He left on Tuesday. (leave-REDUP-3P DAT-Tuesday 3OUM.)
  • - marks location, e.g. Mun l'ümu nä. I study at a school. (study-1P DAT-school 1SG.)
  • - marks time or place of destination, e.g. Pa l'ugu pa. He goes to a house. (go-3P DAT-house 3OUM.)
  • - marks verbs in imperfective copular constructions, e.g. Seon lilei sonlein. I was reading a book. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-read-GER GEN-book.)
  • - marks patients of causative verbs, e.g. Re äb'n pagäipi parä. He made me run. (give-REDUP-3P DAT-1SG run-GER 3OUM-3WUM.)
  • - is used after prepositions governed by the dative case, e.g. Soirn äbee lidango ci nä. I eat with my family. (eat-1P with DAT-family DEF.ART 1SG.)

The cases may be applied to nouns, pronouns and adjectives, the latter agreeing in case with the noun they describe. However, case prefixes are also applied to verbs to form a number of tenses - the genitive conveys the meaning of past, while the dative conveys the meaning of future/irrealis (see 15 Verb tenses).

The direct case is unmarked, while genitive and dative marking depends on the animacy of the noun. Animate nouns (class 3), non-set personal pronouns, as well as all verbs are marked with one set of prefixes, inanimate nouns (class 4 and 5) are marked with another, and set nouns (class 1 and 2) and pronouns are marked with yet another set. Attributive adjectives take the same set of prefixes as the nouns they describe, cf. semica s'olo of good thing (GEN-thing GEN-good) and nicus r'olo of good person (GEN-person GEN-good). Gerunds take the inanimate prefixes when marked for case, since the animate prefixes are used to indicate tense, e.g. peomu doing (do-GER) → äbpeomu going to do (gerund of future form) (AN.DAT-do-GER), lipeomu at doing (dative of gerund) (INAN.DAT-do-GER).

4.4 Clause-final auxiliaries

Each clause ends with an auxiliary word which encodes the clause's subject and direct object (if there is one), in that order. This is referred to as SDOAUX (Subject Direct Object AUXiliary) and is done using personal pronouns in specific forms. Each pronoun has a default form, which is used in isolation or in SDOAUX in clauses without a direct object. Each pronoun also has a prefixing subject form and a suffixing direct object form. If the subject or direct object in the clause is a pronoun, it only appears in SDOAUX, but if it is a noun, it appears inside the clause in the direct case and is reinforced in SDOAUX using a corresponding pronoun. See 7 Personal pronouns and Appendix 1: SDOAUX.

  • Pasc nä. I go. (go-1P 1SG.)
  • Saesc nälu. I kill you. (go-1P 1SG-2SG.)
  • Saec ecci arän. A man kills me. (kill-3P man 3WUM-1SG.)
  • Saesc ecci närä. I kill a man. (kill-1P man 1SG-3WUM.)
  • Saec momc an miion. A woman kills a child. (kill-3P woman child 3EUM-3EUM.)
  • Saec an momc miion. A child kills a woman. (kill-3P child woman 3EUM-3EUM.)

For first and second person pronouns, two identical pronouns cannot be combined (e.g. 1SG-1SG). In such cases, reflexive constructions are used (see 16.4 Impersonal and reflexive expressions). However, this is not true for third person pronouns, because both can have two different referents (e.g. tararc 3NUM-3NUM).

Although compound SDOAUX are created by simply combining a prefixing and suffixing form of two pronouns, there are several things to be wary of.

  • - 1SG, 1PL, and 3NUM have epenthetic vowels in their suffixing forms (-(ä)n, -(i)s, -(a)rc), which only surface in case of illegal clusters, e.g. 3NM-1SG tad- + -(ä)n → *tadn → tadän.
  • - prefixing 1SG nä- is pronounced ni before consonants, except for the epenthetic glide in 1SG-2SG nälu niawu, e.g. 1SG-3NM nädarc nidarc, 1SG-3EUM näion niyon.
  • - prefixing 1PL si- shortens to s- before plosives, devoicing them if they are voiced, e.g. 1PL-3OUM si- + -pa → *sipa → spa, 1PL-3NM si- + -darc → *sidarc → starc.
  • - combinations äa aä are respelled as e o, e.g. 3WUM-2PL arä- + -al → *aräal → arel, 3OUM-3SM pa- + -äläb → *paäläb → polab.
  • - the suffixing forms undergo umlaut, e.g. 3OM-1PL pab- + -is → *pabis → päbis.

Because both subjects and direct objects take the direct case, some ambiguities may arise and must be resolved through context, e.g. Saesaec cus papa. (kill-REDUP-3P person 3OUM-3OUM.) can mean He killed the person., or The person killed him. Some syncretism within compound SDOAUX occurs, which must be disambiguated through context or spelling, e.g. 3OM-3OUM pab- + -pa → pabpa pappá, 3OM-3OM pab- + -ba → pabba pappá.

SDOAUX are omitted when the verb in the clause is a copula or a locative copula and the subject is a first or second person pronoun, since these verbs conjugate for number as well as for person. The same is true for SDOAUX which repeat in subsequent clauses - only the final one must be used, provided that the pronouns apply to the same referents. Intransitive sentences with the demonstrative gä as a subject also do not require an auxiliary.

  • Seon emi. I am here. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-here.)
  • Ar emi pa. It is here. (LOC.COP-3P DAT-here 3OUM.)
  • Ägä carst, mun nä. I lose, but I learn. (but lose-1P, learn-1P 1SG.)
  • Orsaesc momc a näion, saesc an cän näion. If I kill the woman, I kill the child. (GEN-kill-1SG woman DEF.ART 1SG-3EUM, kill-1SG child DEF.ART 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Lä doils gä. It's hard. (COP-3P hard DEM.)

There are cases of multiple subjects or direct objects in a clause (x and y, x or y). If pronouns are included therein, they are not omitted in favor of SDOAUX. The SDOAUX in such cases always takes the plural (1P and 2P) or marked (3P) form, with the NSWEO determined by the first noun mentioned.

  • Äscest ä nä, ciulu si. You and me are going to die. (DAT-die-1P and 1SG, 2SG 1PL.)
  • Elliciu dar ciulu, dar ecci co la. You or the man knows. (know-2P or 2SG, or man DEF.ART 2PL.)
  • Eli äb ecci, momc e leüt, ugu ariri. A man and a woman see a tree and a house. (see-3P and man, woman and tree, house 3WM-3WM.)

5 Articles


5.1 Definite article

The language has a definite article, but no indefinite article. It stems from the demonstrative gä this which is devoiced to cä and further modified based on noun class:

  • - through dissimilation, cä becomes ca after front vowels and class-specific endings are appended
  • - the form is leveled to the S form co after front vowels, and after back vowels, to the W form ci except the E form cän
  • - an epenthetic neutral vowel a is added at the beginning of the article after consonants except /ɰ̃/; the rest of the article is clipped

The resultant forms are as follows:

Post-front Post-back Post-C
NSWO co co ci chi a a
E co co cän chan a a

If the preceding word has no class (definite articles may appear directly after negative articles), the NSWO form is used.

The vowel-initial article is treated as an enclitic, which is reflected in the pronunciation, e.g. sir a hin a. Articles are never stressed.

The definite article is placed directly after the head noun, before descriptors such as adjectives. It does not inflect for number or case.

  • siob a soniob the fast lion (lion DEF.ART fast)
  • siosiob a sononiob the fast lions (lion-REDUP DEF.ART fast-REDUP)
  • an cän r'ecci co spagpeo the child of the walking man (child DEF.ART GEN-man DEF.ART GEN-walk-GER)
  • anan cän r'eccigi co spagpeo the children of the walking men (child-REDUP DEF.ART GEN-man-REDUP DEF.ART GEN-walk-GER)

The article is primarily used:

  • - with set nouns, e.g. nid a the sun, bis a the world, doespaü co the four seasons
  • - with interrogatives in question clauses, e.g. Piellin lilä co sünüüsü nipa näpa. I don't know where he lives. (NEG-know-1P where DEF.ART GEN-live-GER GEN-3OUM 1SG-3OUM.)
  • - in place of possessive pronouns or demonstratives, particularly when they have been previously mentioned or are obvious from context, e.g. Ren lisurs a mirn närc. I hurt the (my) head. (give-REDUP-1P DAT-head DEF.ART pain 1SG-3NUM.), Edest micobbaba ci näpa. I finished the task (that we all know of). (end-REDUP-1P task DEF.ART 1SG-3OUM.)
  • - when referring to a specific referent that has previously been mentioned, e.g. Eolin ugu näion. Ar do ugu cän mion. I saw a house. The (that) house was big. (see-REDUP-1P house 1SG-3EUM. COP-REDUP-3P big house DEF.ART 3EUM.)
  • - after head nouns of defining clauses, e.g. cus a s'elii r'än the person who sees me (person DEF.ART GEN-see-GER GEN-1SG)
  • - for superlatives when used after adjectives, cf. milm a sono the fast cat (cat DEF.ART fast), milm sono ci the fastest cat (cat fast DEF.ART)
5.2 Negative article

The negative article is indefinite and is the equivalent of the English no ~. It stems from the abbreviated gerund of the negative locative copula nü(r) → nü, essentially meaning lacking. For the most part, its forms mirror the patterns of the definite article, but after /ɰ̃/, an is used.

Post-front Post-back Post-C
NSWO no no nüi nüi an an
E no no nüän nüan an an

The negative article is used:

  • - to negate sentences with an indefinite subject or direct object, cf. Pielin ugu cän näion. I don't see the house. (NEG-see-1P house DEF.ART 1SG-3EUM.) and Elin ugu nüän näion. lit. I see no house. = I don't see a house. (see-1P house NEG.ART 1SG-3EUM.).
  • - to form certain indefinite pronouns, e.g. mican nothing (see 10.1 Negative article - none/some)
  • - to negate adjectives, when placed after them instead of after the noun, cf. ugu nüän do no big house (house NEG.ART big) and ugu do nüi not-big house (house big NEG.ART)

The negative article is used with unmarked nouns, with two exceptions:

  • - with class 1 nouns used not as unique referents, e.g. Lä gä sosolt s'agaci bibis an paba. No world is empty of questions. (COP-3P DEM GEN-darkness GEN-question world-REDUP NEG.ART 3OM.)
  • - with abstract class 5 nouns used as instances, e.g. Saesaec mimirn an misda tadän. No pain has killed me yet. (kill-REDUP-3P pain-REDUP NEG.ART still 3NM-1SG.)

6 Nouns


6.1 Animacy

Each noun belongs to one of five animacy classes, which determines the case prefixes it takes, what first and second pronouns can be used to refer to it, and most importantly, how it inflects for number.

◆ Class 1

Class 1 consists of unique nouns which only have one possible referent in their most general sense. This includes almost exclusively entities found in nature, such as celestial objects. In their base meanings, they are typically followed by a definite article. They are reduplicated when not referring to them as unique referents, typically in metaphorical, imaginary, or fictional settings.

7 Personal pronouns


7.1 First and second person pronouns

Since first and second person pronouns practically never have inanimate referents, there is only a distinction between singular, plural and set forms. If an inanimate object is referred to in first or second person, it is assumed to be personified and is treated as animate.

Singular pronouns are unmarked, while the others are derived from the singular prefixing forms with the word bis all marking set pronouns and boa many marking plural pronouns. The first person plural pronoun stems from a separate root.

7.3 Possessives

The genitive pronouns double as possessive determiners. Similarly to articles and demonstratives, possessive determiners are placed directly after the head noun, before other descriptors, e.g. scaian r'än olo my good friend (friend GEN-1SG good).

When the possessor is the subject of the sentence, personal possessive pronouns are usually replaced with either the definite article, especially when referring to body parts, or the reflexive pronoun. In third person, genitive pronouns can be used to distinguish between one's own and someone else's possession.

  • Panst ümpüi co mienarn närc. I felt rain in the (my) eyes. (feel-REDUP-1P DAT-eye DEF.ART rain 1SG-1NUM.)
  • Üi l'ugu r'usa pa. He went to his (own) house. (go-REDUP-3P DAT-house GEN-REFL 3OUM.)
  • Üi l'ugu nipa pa. He went to his (someone else's) house. (go-REDUP-3P DAT-house GEN-3OUM 3OUM.)

Genitive pronouns can only function as possessive determiners. To use possessive pronouns, the impersonal demonstrative gä is appended before a genitive pronoun, as in gä r'än mine. This construction works with nouns too.

  • Lä gä orlu an gän mion. This child is yours. (COP-3P DEM GEN-2SG child DEM 3EUM.)
  • Coons basadan orscaian bans tiodiol bans nä scä r'än näion. I used a friend's phone because I'd lost mine. (use-REDUP-1P phone GEN-friend for lose-REDUP-GER by 1SG GEN-DEM GEN-1SG 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Ar dert r'anan cän adainstac a arä, piireli gä diinonop a. The accident was the children's fault, not the father's. (COP-REDUP-3P fault GEN-child-REDUP DEF.ART accident DEF.ART 3WUM, not DEM GEN-parent-REDUP DEF.ART.)
7.4 Reflexive pronouns

The reflexive pronoun usa usa stems from the word usa self, heart, identity and has no SDOAUX forms. When it is used as the direct object, the SDOAUX only features the subject form (see 14.8 Impersonal and reflexive expressions).

The genitive form of the reflexive pronoun is r'usa nusa, while the dative form is äbsa assa (note the irregular pronunciation).

8 Interrogatives


8.1 lä, cuä - what, who

The basic interrogative lä la means what and other interrogatives are derived from it. Despite its ending, lä and compounds ending in the word belong to the neutral class. It is classed as inanimate. It does not inflect for number and always uses unmarked pronouns. Unlike in English, the word cannot be used attributively; for this purpose, the word sonlä is used (see 8.2 sonlä, lilä - which, whence, where, whither).

  • Dapeom an cän lä mipa? What is the child doing? (INT-do-3P child DEF.ART what 3EUM-3OUM?)
  • Piellin lä co s'intisun scä spa. We don't know what started this. (NEG-know-1P what DEF.ART GEN-start-REDUP-GER GEN-DEM 1PL-3OUM.)

The word cuä qua means who and is derived from cus lä what person. Like lä, it is classed as neutral and does not inflect for number, taking only unmarked pronouns. Unlike lä, it is animate. The genitive is nicuä niqua whose, of whom, from whom, and the dative is äbcuä acqua to whom.

  • D'ar üta scä cuä pa? Who said this? (INT-COP-REDUP-3P like GEN-DEM who 3OUM?)
  • Dadobeel äbcuä ciulu? Who are you talking to? (INT-talk-2P DAT-who 2SG?)
  • Dadobeel nicuä ciulu? Who are you talking about? (INT-talk-2P GEN-who 2SG?)
  • Dalä gä nicuä ugu gän mion? Whose is this house? (INT-COP-3P DEM GEN-who house DEM 3EUM?)
8.2 sonlä, lilä - which, whence, where, whither

The words sonlä sounia and lilä lila have various meanings. Firstly, they can be used as the genitive and dative forms of lä what. Secondly, they can refer to location, meaning whence (from where) and where (at what place), whither (to what place), respectively. Both words are inanimate.

  • D'üic emimi rot sonlä ciulu? With what (mode of transportation) did you go there? (INT-go-REDUP-2P DAT-there through GEN-what 2SG?)
  • D'ar caian lilä äblu mion? What do you like? (INT-LOC.COP-3P fondness DAT-what DAT-2SG 3EUM?)
  • Danänälc sonlä ciulu? Where do you come from? (INT-come-REDUP-2P whence 2SG?)
  • D'äbseon lidoreo semico lilä? Where can I find happiness? (INT-DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-find-GER GEN-happiness where?)
  • D'ar ugu orlu lilä mion? Where is your house? (INT-LOC.COP-3P house GEN-2SG where 3EUM?)

The other usage of sonlä is after nouns, in which case it means which ~, what ~, what kind of ~. Similarly to possessives, it can only be used attributively, and to use it nominally, the demonstrative gä must precede it. In this sense, it could be used as the English how.

  • Danänälc stü sonlä ciulu? What place do you come from? (INT-come-REDUP-2P GEN-place which 2SG?)
  • Dalä ugu orlu gä sonlä pa? Which (one) is your house? (INT-COP-3P house GEN-2SG DEM which 3OUM?)
  • Dalä gä sonlä ugu orlu mion? How is your house? (INT-COP-3P DEM which house GEN-2SG 3EUM?)
8.3 imi, baläns, boalä - when, why, how, how many, how much

The word imi imi means when and is derived from limis lä at what time. It is inanimate and belongs to the neutral class.

  • D'edet imi usa pa? When did he die? (INT-end-REDUP-3P when self 3OUM?)
  • Lälä mican rütnü ellii s'imi co säbnämisdal ris. I want to know when we will come back. (COP-1SG nothing without know-GER GEN-when DEF.ART GEN-DAT-return-GER GEN-1PL.)

The word baläns valans means both why and how, and is derived from bans lä for what. It is inanimate and belongs to the neutral class. In case of ambiguity, why can be expressed as bans coüntinst sonlä for what reason and how can be expressed as rot sorot sonlä by what way.

  • D'edet baläns usa pa? How did he die? (INT-end-REDUP-3P how self 3OUM?)
  • D'äbcaültaciu baläns ciulu? Why do you have to leave? (INT-DAT-leave-REDUP-2P why 2SG?)

The word boalä vuala means how many, how much and is derived from boa lä what many. It is inanimate and belongs to the neutral class. It can be used attributively, nominally and adverbially. It can also be used to quantify adjectives and adverbs when placed after them.

  • D'ar anan boalä äblu mionon? How many children do you have? (INT-LOC.COP-3P child-REDUP how.many DAT-2SG 3EM?)
  • D'elliciu boalä co storereo nipa cipa? Do you know how much he won? (INT-know-2P how.much DEF.ART GEN-win-REDUP-GER GEN-3OUM 2SG-3OUM?)
  • Dalä nantee boalä stadag go läläb? How strong is this fire? (INT-COP-3P strong how.much fire-REDUP DEM 3SM?)
  • Daliu mican rütnü gä boalä? How much do you want this? (INT-COP-2SG nothing without DEM how.much?)

9 Demonstratives


9.1 Basic demonstrative

The basic demonstrative stems from the word gä ja, which, like lä, belongs to the neutral class despite its ending. This demonstrative can be used attributively in which case it acts much like the definite article, agreeing with the NSWEO of the head noun and being placed directly after it, before any descriptors, e.g. ecci gi bog this tall man. It does not inflect for number or case.

The base form gä is used for the neutral class; for other NSWEO, class-specific endings are appended.

North gäl jau
South go go
West gi ji
East gän jan
Neutral gä ja

The neutral gä can also be used as a demonstrative pronoun, being the equivalent of English this. It can be used in impersonal constructions, acting similarly to the English it and one (see 14.8 Impersonal and reflexive expressions). Intransitive sentences with gä as the subject do not require SDOAUX. In some cases, gä is practically interchangeable with the neutral pronoun pa.

  • Lä ugu gä. This is a house. (COP-3P house DEM.)
  • Lä ugu pa. It is a house. (COP-3P house 3OUM.)

The demonstrative is also used to nominalize various constructions.

  • - possessives, e.g. gä r'än mine (DEM GEN-1SG)
  • - ordinals, e.g. gä ipric the third one (DEM ORD-3)
  • - genitive phrases, e.g. läl gä sosolt be empty (COP DEM GEN-emptiness)
  • - entire clauses, e.g. gä storereo r'is the fact that we won (DEM GEN-win-REDUP-GER GEN-1PL)
  • - quantified phrases, e.g. gä rea more of it (DEM more), gä min four of it (DEM 4)
9.2 Proximal and distal demonstratives

Except for the words mi here and mimi there (see 9.3 Other forms - scä, eligä, boagä, mins, mi, mimi), there is no distinction between proximal and distal demonstratives. However, the adjectives müi müi near and dans dans far may be placed directly before the head to convey proximity (in this context müi and dans do not participate in adjective agreement).

  • Lä do müi ugu gän mion. This house is big. (COP-3P big near house DEM 3EUM.)
  • Saesaesc dans momc gän näion. I killed that woman. (kill-REDUP-1P far woman DEM 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Piiliubamt müi bamt scä mion. This kind of flower doesn't blossom. (NEG-blossom-3P near flower such 3EUM.)
  • Orpeomc dans mins cipa. Don't do it like that. (GEN-do-2P far thus 2SG-3OUM.)
9.3 Other forms - scä, eligä, boagä, mins, mi, mimi

Since gä is the demonstrative equivalent of lä, several demonstratives can be derived from interrogatives by replacing the latter with the former. However, the meanings of the resultant forms are not necessarily predictable from the interrogatives.

The form scä scha is the genitive form of the demonstrative gä, and is also used to mean such, this kind of. Like its interrogative equivalent sonlä, it can only be used attributively, and gä must precede it when used nominally. The form eligä elija is the dative form of gä (note the irregular case marking), and is also used to mean then, at that time, and. This word can be further inflected into the genitive s'eligä hielija from then and the dative l'eligä lelija till then. Finally, boagä vuaja is the demonstrative equivalent of boalä, and means this much, this many.

  • Ellin dü scä näpa. I know its place. (know-1P place GEN-DEM 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Ebnüä lipeomu semicobbaba scä. I can't do such tasks. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-do-GER GEN-task such.)
  • Ar caian eligä äb'n mion. I like this. (LOC.COP-3P fondness DAT-DEM DAT-1SG 3EUM.)
  • Rot säicciu eligä an ciion. You should kill a child then. (by kill-2P then child 2SG-3EUM.)
  • Lälä mican rütnü caio boagä. I want this much love. (COP-1SG nothing without love this.much.)
  • Lä do boagä leüt a mion. The tree is this big. (COP-3P big this.much tree DEF.ART 3EUM.)

The word mins miens means thus, like this, so and is derived from bans mi for here. Apart from its demonstrative use, it is also the word meaning yes.

  • Lä nantee mins stadag a läläb. The fire is so strong. (COP-3P strong so fire-REDUP DEF.ART 3SM.)
  • Dadobeel mins baläns ciulu? Why are you talking like this? (INT-talk-2P thus why 2SG.)
  • Mins, rättädä mins pan. Yes, he taught me thus. (yes, teach-REDUP-3P thus 3OUM-1SG.)

The word mi mi means here and is derived from müi close. Its reduplicated form mimi mimi means there. Both words act only as nouns, and to use them adverbially, case marking must be used. The genitive forms semi hiemi and semimi hiemimi mean hence (from here) and thence (from there), respectively. The dative forms emi emi and emimi emimi are irregular and mean here (at this place), hither (to this place) and there (at that place), thither (to that place), respectively.

  • Lä dü olo mi arä. It's a good place here. (COP-3P place good here 3WUM.)
  • Eb'üint semi si. We need to get out of here. (DAT-go-REDUP-1P GEN-here 1PL.)
  • Piäbpa momc a emimi mion. The woman will not go there. (NEG-DAT-go-3P woman DEF.ART DAT-there 3EUM.)
  • Ar nid a emimi läb. The sun is there. (LOC.COP-3P sun DEF.ART DAT-there 3SUM.)

10 Indefinite pronouns and adjectives


10.1 Negative article - none/some

Negative pronouns are formed with the negative article. The article by itself translates to English no ~ and can be used to negate indefinite sentences (see 5.2 Negative article). Verbs are not negated when used with negative articles in their original meaning. Negating the verb reverses the meaning of the article to some ~, since a phrase like Piipeoms mican näpa. lit. I don't do nothing. is interpreted as It is not true that I do nothing. = I do something.

  • Lä do mins ugu nüän mion. No house is that big. (COP-3P big thus house NEG.ART 3OUM.)
  • Saesaesc momc an nänon. I didn't kill any woman. (kill-REDUP-1P woman NEG.ART 1SG-3EM.)
  • Piidobeen äbpa r'ecci no nä. I talk to him about some man. (NEG-talk-1P DAT-3OUM GEN-man NEG.ART 1SG.)
  • Piidobeen äb'ecci no nipa nä. I talk to some man about him. (NEG-talk-1P DAT-man NEG.ART GEN-3OUM 1SG.)

Other indefinite pronouns formed with the negative article are presented below.

cusan cusan no one/someone no person (cus person + an NEG.ART)
linür linür (to) nowhere/somewhere at/to not-being (li DAT + nür NEG.LOC.COP-GER)
sünür hünür from nowhere/somewhere from not-being (so GEN + nür NEG.LOC.COP-GER)

Case marking for these pronouns is the same as for the original nouns, so for instance the genitive form nicusan mirrors the irregularity of the genitive form of cus - nicus.

  • Üint linür nä. I went nowhere. (go-REDUP-1P DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-GER 1SG.)
  • Piinänäln sünür nä. I came from somewhere. (NEG-come-REDUP-1P GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-GER 1SG.)
  • Ar üta semican ecci co arä. The man said nothing. (COP-REDUP-3P like GEN-nothing man DEF.ART 3WUM.)
  • Piidobeen äbcusan semican nä. I talk to someone about something. (NEG-talk-1P DAT-nobody GEN-nothing 1SG.)
  • Ar mican äb'n pa. I have nothing. (LOC.COP-3P nothing DAT-1SG 3OUM.)

The meaning of some ~ can also be expressed with generic nouns, for instance mica thing (something), cus person (someone), dü place (somewhere), mis time (sometime), rot way (somehow). Another alternative is using the adjective da some, certain.

  • Eolin l'ugu cän mica näpa. I saw something in the house. (see-REDUP-1P DAT-house DEF.ART thing 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Adudut cus pan. Someone hit me. (hit-REDUP-3P person 3OUM-1SG.)
  • Ar tols stü säbsun bans nä licoütun äb'n tarc. I need somewhere to think. (LOC.COP-3P need GEN-place GEN-DAT-LOC.COP-GER by 1SG DAT-think-GER DAT-1SG 3NUM.)

  • Räbibit rot sorotba stalotba leüt parä. He cut a tree with some tool. (cut-REDUP-3P by GEN-tool GEN-some tree 3OUM-3WUM.)
  • Padadals odnupaim senlisadainst lida näion. I got an invitation to some event. (receive-REDUP-1P invitation GEN-DAT-event DAT-some 1SG-3EUM.)

To use indefinite pronouns in negative sentences, the phrasing is changed from, e.g. sometimes ~ not to not always, using the word piireli not and the word bis every.

  • Räb piireli äbnop ebbis äbanan r'usa caio aräläb. Not all parents love their children. = Some parents don't love their children. (give-3P not DAT-parent DAT-every DAT-child-REDUP GEN-REFL love 3WUM-3SUM.)
  • Doens popba piireli limibis nä. Not always do I feel well. = Sometimes I don't feel well. (feel-1P well not DAT-always 1SG.)
  • Lä täo piireli imbis pa. Not everything is right. = Something isn't right. (COP-3P right not DAT-everything 3OUM.)
10.2 bis - every

The word bis vis has various uses and meanings. It can be used as a class 1 noun to mean the world or to convey spatial relations, with the meaning of within, among, inside. It can be used as an adjective to mean all, every, each, or as a pronoun to mean everything, everyone. For disambiguation, the adjectival phrases mica bisica mica vihíca every thing and cus bisus cus visus every person can be used.

Other than that, bis is used as an enclitic with two pronouns and two nouns.

Word Meaning Etymology
sonbis sonvis from everywhere from every where (sonlä whence + bis every)
libis livis (to) everywhere to every where (lilä where, whither + bis every)
mibis mivis always, at all times at all times (li DAT + mis time + bis every)
retbis yeppis in all ways, completely (by) all ways (rot way + bis every)

Note that used as an enclitic, bis causes umlaut in retbis but not in sonbis, because of the intervening lä which is then elided. The words sonbis and libis appear to be genitive and dative forms of bis, but they are actually clipped forms of sonlä bis and lilä bis, respectively. The word bis itself uses set case prefixes - the genitive diibis daivis and the dative imbis envis. The word mibis is used to mean always in the sense of at all times. For the meaning forever, the word elit elit (DAT-end) is more appropriate.

  • Äsceget an bis mion. Every child must die. (DAT-die-REDUP-3P child every 3EUM.)
  • Stagbee stag mibis läb. Fire is always burning. (burn-3P fire always 3SUM.)
  • Ar ugu libis mion. There are houses everywhere. (LOC.COP-3P house DAT-everywhere 3EUM.)

Similarly to the negative article, bis is used with unmarked nouns, with two exceptions:

  • - with class 1 nouns used not as unique referents, e.g. Lä olo ninid bibis läläb. Every sun is good. (COP-3P good sun-REDUP every-REDUP 3SM.)
  • - with abstract class 5 nouns used as instances, e.g. Re caioio bibis äb'n mirn pagson s'eligä tadarc. Every love has hurt me since then. (give-REDUP-3P love-REDUP every-REDUP DAT-1SG pain since GEN-then 3NM-3NUM.)
10.3 Potential form - any

In most cases in which English would use pronouns such as anything or whatever, the pronoun is synonymous with some or every, e.g. Is anyone (someone) interested?, I can do anything (everything). Therefore, there is no need for such pronouns in this language. However, in subjunctive clauses, a plain interrogative is used and the predicate is conjugated into the potential form (see 15.7 Potential).

  • Äbseosc lipeomu sonlä, spal emimi. Whatever you do, don't go there. (DAT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-do-GER GEN-what, GEN-go-2P DAT-there.)
  • Äbseon lidiii s'ugu sonlä, äb'ar caian eb'ion äb'n mion. Whichever house I choose, I'll like it. (DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-choose-GER GEN-house which, DAT-LOC.COP-3P fondness DAT-3EUM DAT-1SG 3EUM.)
  • Äbseon l'äbeecuns äbcuä, dosue mibis r'än pa. Whoever I talk to, they always leave me. (DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-talk-GER DAT-who, leave-3P always GEN-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Äb'ar cuä linäl äb'n pa, lälä mican rütnü uloo nipa. Whoever comes to me, I want to hug them. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P who DAT-come-GER DAT-1SG 3OUM, COP-1SG nothing without hug-GER GEN-3OUM.)
10.4 cago and darn and their contractions

The adjectives cago same and darn other change their meaning depending on the definiteness of the noun they modify. cago means the same when definite and an identical when indefinite, and darn can mean the other when definite and another when indefinite.

  • Nie l'ugu cän licago pa. He lives in the same house. (live-3P DAT-house DEF.ART DAT-same 3OUM.)
  • Nie l'ugu licago pa. He lives in an identical house. (live-3P DAT-house DAT-same 3OUM.)
  • Cusosos äblu lein a darn näion. I gave you the other book. (offer-REDUP-1P DAT-2SG book DEF.ART other 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Cusosos äblu lein darn näion. I gave you another book. (offer-REDUP-1P DAT-2SG book other 1SG-3EUM.)

This dichotomy is also observed in negative sentences, where the verb is negated when definite phrases are involved, and the adjective is negated when indefinite phrases are involved. Note that when the meaning is no identical and no other, it is the noun that is followed by a negative article.

  • Piinie l'ugu cän licago pa. He doesn't live in the same house. (NGE-live-3P DAT-house DEF.ART DAT-same 3OUM.)
  • Nie l'ugu licago nüi pa. He doesn't live in an identical house. (live-3P DAT-house DAT-same NEG.ART 3OUM.)
  • Ada ugu nüän cago mion. No identical house exists. (exist-3P house NEG.ART same 3EUM.)
  • Piicusosos äblu lein a darn näion. I didn't give you the other book. (NEG-offer-REDUP-1P DAT-2SG book DEF.ART other 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Cusosos äblu lein darn an näion. I didn't give you another book. (offer-REDUP-1P DAT-2SG book other NEG.ART 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Gou boagä lein an darn min. No other book changed me this much. (change-REDUP-3P this.much book NEG.ART other 3EUM-1SG.)

11 Adjectives and adverbs


11.1 Placement and characteristics of adjectives

Since the language is exclusively head-initial, adjectives always follow nouns they modify. If an article, demonstrative, or possessive pronoun is used, the adjective follows it. This does not apply to nouns used in the genitive case to express possession.

  • roträbit motit a sharp knife (knife sharp)
  • roträbit a motit the sharp knife (knife DEF.ART sharp)
  • roträbit gi motit this sharp knife (knife DEM sharp)
  • roträbit r'än motit my sharp knife (knife GEN-1SG sharp)
  • roträbit motit r'ecci a man's sharp knife (knife sharp GEN-man)

Adjectives cannot be used attributively with pronouns, as in *nä dane stupid me.

Adjectives are noun-like and have no predicative forms. They are used with the copula, similarly to nouns. Adjectives used predicatively do not participate in ending agreement (see 11.2 Agreement of adjectives with nouns) or number agreement.

  • Lä bee olee läb. It's good light. (COP-3P light good 3SUM.)
  • Lä olo bee läb. Light is good. (COP-3P good light 3SUM.)
  • Lä nantee ecci arä. A man is strong. (COP-3P strong man 3WUM.)
  • Lä nantee eccigi ari. Men are strong. (COP-3P strong man-REDUP 3WM.)

Except for certain constructions, adjectives cannot be used nominally. Nominalizing them can be done either by using a generic noun like cus person or mica thing, or by using the demonstrative gä.

  • Lä olo mica do pa. Big things are good. (COP-3P good thing big 3OUM.)
  • Pumpoo cugus paimabibi pabän. Mean people annoy me. (annoy-3P person-REDUP mean-REDUP 3OM-1SG.)
  • Piebmirneom gä bilp rot bems. The weak won't survive long. (NEG-DAT-survive DEM weak by long.)

Adjectives have a negative form used in certain constructions described in further sections (see 11.6 Comparison of adjectives and adverbs). The form is created by attaching the negative article directly after the adjective. This is possible because the negative article otherwise directly follows the head noun.

  • bee no olee no good light (light NEG.ART good)
  • bee olee no not good light (light good NEG.ART)

There are certain adjectives which are much more commonly used with a definite article than without one. These are similar to those found in English, including sas main and misc current. Some adjectives may change meaning whether they are definite or indefinite, such as irm the only, lone, cago the same, identical, and darn the other, another. The difference collapses, however, when the adjective is used predicatively, since they do not take the definite article. In that case, the demonstrative can be used to convey the definite meaning of the adjective.

  • espuio ci sas the main entrance (entrance DEF.ART main)
  • selit a misc the current king (king DEF.ART current)
  • ugu cän cago / ugu cago the same house / an identical house (house DEF.ART same / house same)
  • micuu cän darn / micuu darn the other song / another song (song DEF.ART other / song other)
  • an cän irm / an irm the only child / a lone child (child DEF.ART lone / child lone)
  • Lä irm an cän mion. The child is alone. (COP-3P lone child DEF.ART 3EUM.)
  • Lä gä irm an cän mion. The child is the only one. (COP-3P DEM lone child DEF.ART 3EUM.)
11.2 Agreement of adjectives with nouns

Adjectives agree with the nouns they modify in case and number. Case prefixes are applied in agreement with the animacy of the noun.

  • mica olo good thing(s), cus olo good person
  • micaga ololo a good thing, cugus ololo good people
  • semica s'olo of good thing(s), nicus r'olo of good person
  • limicaga l'ololo to a good thing, äbcugus äb'ololo to good people

Contrary to number and case, adjectives do not necessarily have to agree with the noun's class, since each adjective belongs to one of NSWEO. If an adjective does have the same class as the noun it modifies, its ending changes to rhyme with the noun, unless they already do, as in sägo cago same pride. The appended ending starts from the stressed vowel of the noun, except for nouns with antepenultimate stress, whose ending starts with the penultimate vowel, e.g. eus-at, n-oba, mic-oso. Preceding semivowels are included provided they stem from orthographic vowels, e.g. m-üe, ca-ian, but matal-ag.

For consonant-final adjectives, the ending is appended to the end of the adjective. For vowel-final adjectives it replaces the final vowel(s), unless the adjective is monosyllabic in which case an intervening epenthetic r (before front vowels) or l (before back vowels) is placed between the adjective and the ending. Finally, the adjective must be stressed on the same syllable as the noun.

  • mienarn + bilp → mienarn bilparn mienarn viwparn weak rain
  • mica + bis → mica bisica mica vihíca everything
  • lob + cago → lob cagob wov cagov same dog
  • caio + nantee → caio nantaio cayo nantáyo strong love
  • micoso + olo → micoso oloso micoso owoso good truth
  • dü + boa → dü boarü jü vuayű many places
  • cura + tiü → cura tiülura cuna chiwúna few parts

When nouns are reduplicated, the agreeing adjectives are first reduplicated and then have their endings changed, e.g. ecci + müi → eccigi müimüi → eccigi müimigi etchiji müimiji close men. Potential case prefixes are appended using these forms as the base.

When a noun agreeing with an adjective is inflected, the adjective inflects regularly, using its rhyming direct form as the base, e.g. ca da ca da some side → lica lida licá lidá to some side, not *lica lidarica lica lidayíca. Moreover, pairs of agreeing nouns and adjectives have fixed stress when case prefixes are applied, e.g. moco spuoloco moco spuowóco dry tongue → somoco sospuoloco somóco sospuowóco of dry tongue.

11.3 Placement and characteristics of adverbs

Adverbs do not have NSWEO and exhibit no agreement. Similarly to adjectives, adverbs follow the word they modify. Adverbs can modify adjectives, verbs, or other adverbs. When an adverb modifies a verb, it generally does not directly follow it, unless it is emphasized, because usually the verb is directly followed by the subject.

  • Lä bilp roba beebee co läläb. The light is very weak. (COP-3P weak very light-REDUP DEF.ART 3SM.)
  • Mu an cän popba mion. The child is learning well. (learn-3P child DEF.ART well 3EUM.)
  • Stagbee stadag a rot nantee enae läläb. The fire is burning quite strongly. (burn-3P fire-REDUP DEF.ART by strong quite 3SM.)

Adverbials are typically placed between the subject and the direct object. Although sentences are usually verb-initial, adverbial phrases are sometimes placed at the beginning of a sentence, especially if they are long and introducing them first is desired by the speaker. This fronting can also be done as a form of disambiguation, emphasizing that the adverbial refers to the entire clause, not the verb itself.

  • Eligä misc, äb'et rot a arä. And now, the path will end. (then now, DAT-end-3P path DEF.ART 3WUM.)
  • Üint misada l'ugu nipa nä. Today, I went to his house. (go-REDUP-1P today DAT-house GEN-3OUM 1SG.)
  • Rot semirn, edet usa pa. Unfortunately, he died. (through sadness, end-REDUP-3P self 3OUM.)
  • Edet rot semirn usa pa. He died unfortunately. (end-REDUP-3P through sadness self 3OUM.)
11.4 Conversion of adjectives into adverbs

The preposition rot by, through can be placed in front of adjectives to turn them into adverbs, e.g. sono quick → rot sono quickly. While this preposition typically requires the following word to be in the genitive case, there is no case marking if rot is used as an adverbializer.

11.5 Adverbs of degree

To convey excessiveness, the word badans vadans overdo, excessive(ly) can be used as an adverb. To express insufficiency, the word bamüi vamüi underdo, insufficient(ly) is used. These stem from the combination of the verb ba *do and the adjectives dans far and müi near.

  • Lä milc badans ugu cän mion. The house is too small. (COP-3P small excessively house DEF.ART 3EUM.)

11.6 Comparison of adjectives and adverbs

For most adjectives and adverbs, there is no inflection involved with comparison. To express the comparative degree, the noun that is being compared to is used in the dative case and placed after the adjective/adverb. When there is no compared noun, the demonstrative gä is used. The adjective/adverb can be affirmative to express more or negative to express less.

  • ecci sono ebmilm man faster than a cat (man fast DAT-cat)
  • Lä bems an pü lilein pa. A page is less long than a book. (COP-3P long NEG.ART page DAT-book 3OUM.)
  • Lälä mican rütnü elii rot banil eligä. I want to see more clearly. (COP-1SG nothing without see-GER by clear DAT-DEM.)

This construction means that the beneficiary of an adjective cannot be expressed with the dative case. Instead, the preposition bans is used.

  • Lä paim äb'n pa. He is nicer than me. (COP-3P nice DAT-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Lä paim bans nä pa. He is nice to me. (COP-3P nice for 1SG 3OUM.)

The superlative is formed by placing the definite article directly after the adjective/adverb, whereas normally it would be placed directly after the head noun.

  • momc dane co the dumbest woman (woman stupid DEF.ART)
  • Dobeencan rot uso ci nä. I spoke the most truthfully. (talk-REDUP-1P by true DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Lä mica uso nüi co imbis a pa. This is the least true thing in the world. (COP-3P thing true NEG.ART DEF.ART DAT-world DEF.ART 3OUM.)

  • pü e bot, e sodi, lü 1110 pages (page and 1000, and 100, 10)
  • ä bota, ä sodia, lü spü 1110 pages (and 1000-NOM, and 100-NOM, 10 GEN-page)

12.3 Occurrences, multiples, fractions

The number of occurrences can be expressed with the class 1 noun iis time, occurrence. Since the noun belongs to class 1, it is reduplicated whenever the number of occurrences is more than one. A single occurrence can be expressed as iis iis/iis diiis one time or just iis a time.

  • iis iis (time 1) / iis diiis (1-NOM GEN-time) / iis (time) one time (noun)
  • iisiis pic (time-REDUP 3) / pica diiis (3-NOM GEN-time) three times (noun)
  • iis itriu eighth time (time ORD-8)
  • iis s'igrä which time (in order) (time ORD-which)
  • iis scä which time (time which)
  • store diiisiis min (winner GEN-time-REDUP 4) / store diimina diiis (winner GEN-4-NOM GEN-time) four time winner
  • store diiis s'inst first time winner (winner GEN-time GEN-start)

Adverbial phrases are formed with the preposition rot.

  • rot diiis iis (by GEN-time 1) / rot diiis diiis (by GEN-1-NOM GEN-time) / rot diiis (by GEN-time) once, one time (adverb)
  • rot diiisiis pic (by GEN-time-REDUP 3) / rot diipica diiis (by GEN-3-NOM GEN-time) thrice, three times (adverb)
  • rot diiis boalä how many times (by GEN-time how.many)
  • rot diiis s'oüt for the second time (by GEN-time GEN-following)
  • rot diiis s'igrä for which time (in order) (by GEN-time ORD-which)

Multiples are expressed with the noun püt(a) nature, manner used as a prefix. Like any prefix, it is subject to umlaut. The resultant words are adjectives, invariant to number or NSWEO, but taking case prefixes which agree in animacy with the noun. Once again, adverbial forms are formed with rot.

  • pütiis single (manner-1)
  • pütalä what-fold (manner-what)
  • saecuguic pütasab double murder (murder-REDUP manner-2)
  • Sancansän sonleüt spütäpic nä. I wrote about a triple tree. (write-REDUP-1P GEN-tree GEN-manner-3 1SG.)
  • rot pütämin quadruply (by manner-4)

  • saban half (from saba two + -n DIM)

13 Conjunctions and prepositions


13.1 Usage of noun cases

Genitive and dative marking is used in certain cases in which English would use prepositions. The genitive case, apart from encoding possession, can be used to mean from, or about.

  • bee sostag the light of fire (light GEN-fire)
  • Näln semimi nä. I come from there. (come-1P GEN-there 1SG.)
  • Dobeencan semienarn si. We talked about rain. (talk-REDUP-1P GEN-rain 1PL.)

The dative case marks indirect objects, but also location, destination and time.

  • Ar ecci l'ugu arä. A man is in a house. (LOC.COP-3P man DAT-house 3WUM.)
  • Pa ecci l'ugu arä. A man goes to a house. (go-3P man DAT-house 3WUM.)
  • Dobeenca ecci äbmomc arä. A man talked to a woman. (talk-REDUP-3P man DAT-woman 3WUM.)
  • Äb'elin lipoinpiugu nälu. I'll see you on Friday. (DAT-see-1P DAT-Friday 1SG-2SG.)

The genitive case is also used when a noun is followed by an attributive phrase with the dative case or a preposition, with the exception of äbee and rütnü (see 13.3 Prepositions). In such phrases, the inanimate forms of genitive prefixes are used. If, however, such phrase involves the use of the genitive case, there is no double marking.

  • räbusca s'äbpa a gift to him (gift GEN-DAT-3OUM)
  • räbusca nipa his gift/a gift from him (gift GEN-3OUM)
  • saecuguic senlimissolt a murder at night (murder-REDUP GEN-DAT-nighttime)
  • adaboibois sobans coüntinst an an existence for no reason (existence-REDUP GEN-for reason NEG.ART)
  • eusasat sorot sosoirdasun a death by overeating (death-REDUP GEN-by GEN-eat-far-GER)
13.2 Conjunctions

Conjunctions and prepositions function in a very similar fashion, requiring the following noun to take on specific case marking. The difference between the two categories is that conjunctions cannot be used as suffixes attached to gerunds.

◆ ä(b)/e(l) - and

This conjunction has the meaning of and and is used with the direct case. It stems from dative markers äb- and eli-. The form ä/äb a/av is used before animate and set nouns and pronouns, while e/el e/eu is used elsewhere. The coda consonant in either of the forms is appended if the following word is vowel-initial.

This conjunction is used to link nouns and adjectives. Unlike the English and, this conjunction is used before the first word in a list, meaning literally (in addition) to x, y for x and y. It requires the use of a comma between each of the listed elements. The conjunction can be repeated before every listed element but the last, or just the first one. Its usage before every non-final element is only required when forming numbers.

  • äb ecci, (ä) momc, an a man (and) a woman and a child (and man, [and] woman, child)
  • mica el ognol, milc bad and small thing (thing and bad, small)
  • ä güra diisodi, ä mina diüü, sel 587 (and 5-NOM GEN-100-NOM, and 4-NOM GEN-20-NOM, 7)

This conjunction is unique in that any case marking applied to the nouns and adjectives following it is taken on by the conjunction itself, with the following nouns and adjectives left unmarked. The choice of the form of said case marking depends on the animacy of the first element listed after the conjunction.

  • Lä räbusca r'ä nä, pa gä. This is a gift from me and him. (COP-3P gift GEN-and 1SG, 3OUM DEM.)
  • Üint l'e pil torsil, rio leübio nä. I went to the cold north and the hot south. (go-REDUP-1P DAT-and north cold, south hot 1SG.)

This conjunction can only link nouns, pronouns, and adjectives, not verbs or clauses. For that purpose, one must either use words like eligä and, then, or simply concatenate two clauses without any additional marking.

  • Üituin ugu r'usa nä, eligä soisoirn star närc. I got home and ate food. (reach-REDUP-1P house GEN-REFL 1SG, then eat-REDUP-1P food 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Eoli an, saesaec paion. He saw a child and killed it. (see-REDUP-3P child, kill-REDUP-3P 3OUM-3EUM.)

Although not applied to finite verbs, it can be used with gerunds, since they function as nouns.

  • Äb'ar l'e micuu, domatasue pa. He can sing and dance. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P DAT-and sing-GER, dance-GER 3OUM.)
  • Seon l'e soir, pagpeo. I was eating and walking. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-and eat-GER, walk-GER.)
◆ dar - or

The conjunction dar dar is used to mean (either), or and is used with the direct case. It originates from the word darn meaning other. It is placed between words or clauses, and is preceded by a comma. It can be used before the first of the listed items, in which case it does not require any punctuation to precede it. Placed sentence-initially before a comma, this conjunction has the meaning of otherwise, else, if not.

  • Äb'ar äbläl olo mi, dar mimi paba. Here or there could be good. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P DAT-COP-GER good here, or there 3OM.)
  • (Dar) nälc, dar piinälc ciulu. You (either) come, or you don't. ([or] come-2P, or NEG-come-2P 2SG.)
  • Äbdores nä. Dar, äb'est usa nä. I have to win. Otherwise I will die. (DAT-win-REDUP-1P 1SG. else, DAT-end-1P self 1SG.)

Unlike with ä(b)/e(l), case marking is applied to nouns, pronouns and adjectives following dar, never the conjunction itself.

  • Piorcuseciu dar äblob, dar ebmilm ebmilci cipa. Give it to a dog or to a cute cat. (NEG-GEN-offer-2P or DAT-dog, or DAT-cat DAT-cute 2SG-3OUM.)
  • Lä gä diinonop r'än, dar ürmüe r'än bamt gän mion. This flower is either my mother's or my sister's. (COP-3P DEM GEN-parent-REDUP GEN-1SG, or GEN-sibling GEN-1SG flower DEM 3EUM.)

The preposition can also be used to express the meaning of quite, rather by placing it after an adjective or adverb. This can be done attributively and predicatively. In the latter case the auxiliary verb ba *do is used and the adjective is used nominally as a quasi-direct object, thus it does not participate in NSWEO agreement with the subject noun. The meaning not very can be achieved by negating the verb, but this form cannot be used attributively.

  • mica do dar quite a big thing (thing big or)
  • Ba mienarn a matado dar tarläb. The rain is quite loud. (do-3P rain DEF.ART loud or 3NUM-3SUM.)
  • Piiba leüt a dans dar aräion. The tree is not very far. (NEG-do-3P tree DEF.ART far or 3WUM-3EUM.)

The same construction with the auxiliary ba can be formed using gerunds and nouns as well. This conveys the meaning of do things such as, do or something.

  • Piobans sciti dar nä. I didn't die or anything. (NEG-do-REDUP-1P die-GER or 1SG.)
  • D'äbobal soir dar, col dar ciulu? Do you have to eat or rest or something? (INT-DAT-do-REDUP-2P eat-GER or rest-GER or 2SG?)
  • Daseosc l'elii sosami r'än dar spoinp dar? Were you looking at my tits or ass or something? (INT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-see-GER GEN-ass GEN-1SG or GEN-tits or?)
◆ ägä - but

The conjunction ägä aja is used with the direct case and stems from the proto-form läläl gä, which meant it may be. When placed before nouns, it has the meaning of despite.

  • Caülst s'ugu cän ägä mienarn si. We left the house despite the rain. (leave-REDUP-1P GEN-house DEF.ART but rain 1PL.)
  • Ägä ellii, äbdan mican näpa. Despite knowing, I'll say nothing. (but know-GER, DAT-say-1SG nothing 1SG-3OUM.)

Placed at the beginning of a clause before the verb, ägä means although, but, even though, even if. In this case it is placed in the subordinate clause, since it literally translates to it may be that x, y, which is used to mean any of the following: x, but y, although x, y, even though x, y, even if x, y. For the latter meaning, the main clause is in the future tense.

  • Ägä elin mienarn närc, pielin nid a näläb. I see rain, but I don't see the sun. (but see-1P rain 1SG-1NUM, NEG-see-1P sun DEF.ART 1SG-3SUM.)
  • Ägä äbmadidicciu ciulu, äbdontaciu tarü boalarü cipa. Even though you have to work, you will get a lot of money. (but DAT-work-REDUP-2P 2SG, DAT-get-2P money many 2SG-3OUM.)
  • Ägä äb'ar solt l'uloo orlu tarc, äbseon lica orlu. Even if darkness surrounds you, I'll be by your side. (but DAT-LOC.COP-3P darkness DAT-surround-GER GEN-2SG 3NUM, DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-side GEN-2SG.)

The word ägä can also be used to mean only, just, simply. Note that in this case the following noun does not necessarily have to be in the direct case.

  • Peom ägä ecci co lisionsolt paiman aräion. Only the man makes cookies in December. (make-3P but man DEF.ART DAT-December cookie 3WUM-3EUM.)
  • Peom ecci co ägä lisionsolt paiman aräion. The man makes cookies only in December. (make-3P man DEF.ART DAT-December cookie 3WUM-3EUM.)
  • Peom ecci co lisionsolt ägä paiman aräion. The man makes only cookies in December. (make-3P man DEF.ART DAT-December but cookie 3WUM-3EUM.)

When used in this sense, the conjunction may be preceded by a negative pronoun. Note that adding the negative pronoun may change the verb conjugation, as well as the class of the subject or direct object, resulting in a different SDOAUX. If a pronoun which represents the subject or direct object follows this conjunction, it is used in its direct form and later reinforced in SDOAUX.

  • Elin ägä nal närc. I see only water. (see-1P but water 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Elin mican ägä nal näpa. I see nothing but water. = I see only water. (see-1P nothing but water 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Elin ägä nä nal närc. Only I see water. (see-1P but 1SG water 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Eli cusan ägä nä nal parc. No one but me sees water. = Only I see water. (see-3P nobody but 1SG water 3OUM-3NUM.)
  • Seon ägä nä emi. Only I am here. (LOC.COP-1SG but 1SG DAT-here.)
  • Ar cusan ägä nä emi pa. No one but me is here. = Only I am here. (LOC.COP-3P nobody but 1SG DAT-here 3OUM.)

When using ägä with verbs and adjectives, the auxiliary verb ba do is employed, and the direct object is the verb (in the gerund) or the adjective.

  • Bal ägä soir orlolob circ. You only eat dogs (and you don't do other things). (do-2P but eat-GER GEN-dog-REDUP 2SG-3NUM.)
  • Bans mican ägä sar semirn näpa. I do nothing but suffer. (do-1P nothing but take-GER GEN-pain 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Ba ecci gi ägä e bilp, rapaic arädarc. This man is just weak and pathetic. (do-3P man DEM but and weak, pathetic 3WUM-3NM.)
◆ cagoeli - also

The conjunction cagoeli cagueli is used with the dative case and stems from the combination of cago agree and the dative marker eli. Its usage is similar to that of ägä when used to mean only. Placed before nouns, cagoeli has the meaning of also, even. Used multiple times, it carries the meaning of both x and y in affirmative sentences and neither x nor y in negative sentences. If a subject or direct object pronoun follows this conjunction, it is used in its dative form and reinforced in SDOAUX. The person marking of the verb agrees with the subject even though in such constructions it is not used in the direct case.

  • Girn cagoeli äb'n mililm nädarc. I too/Even I fight cats. (fight-1P also DAT-1SG cat-REDUP 1SG-3NM.)
  • Girn cagoeli ebmililm nädarc. I fight also/even cats. (fight-1P also DAT-cat-REDUP 1SG-3NM.)
  • Pasc cagoeli äb'n cagoeli äb'ecci co l'ugu cän si. Both me and the man go to the house. (go-1P also DAT-1SG also DAT-man DEF.ART DAT-house DEF.ART 1PL.)
  • Elin cagoeli l'ugu nüän cagoeli lileüt an nänon. I see neither a house nor a tree. (see-1P also DAT-house NEG.ART also DAT-tree NEG.ART 1SG-3EM.)

If cagoeli is followed by a phrase in the genitive case or a preposition, a dative prefix precedes said preposition or genitive marker. In such phrases, the inanimate forms of dative prefixes are used. If, however, cagoeli is followed by a phrase in the dative case, there is no double marking, and the meaning must be understood from context.

  • Dobeen cagoeli äb'ecci co nä. I talk to the man as well (as well as to other people). (talk-1P-to also DAT-man DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Piüint cagoeli l'ugu r'än cagoeli lis'ugu r'än nä. I didn't go to my house nor from my house. (NEG-go-REDUP-1P also DAT-house GEN-1SG also DAT-GEN-house GEN-1SG 1SG.)
  • Pasc cagoeli lirütnü ecci co nä. I go without the man as well (as well as without other people). (go-1P also DAT-without man DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Lal cagoeli linistorere cagoeli liniscagart. You are neither winners nor losers. (COP-2PL also DAT-GEN-winner-REDUP also DAT-GEN-loser-REDUP.)

As with ägä, the use of this conjunction with verbs and adjectives requires the use of the auxiliary verb ba do.

  • Bal cagoeli libü stast semico cipa. You also/even look happy (among doing other things). (do-2P also DAT-do-GER GEN-shape GEN-happiness 2SG-3OUM.)
  • Ba ecci co cagoeli l'olo aräläb. The man is also good (among other qualities). (do-3P man DEF.ART also DAT-good 3WUM-3SUM.)
  • Piibans cagoeli lipao cagoeli linäl nääläb. I don't go and don't come either. (NEG-do-1P also DAT-go-GER also DAT-come-GER 1SG-3SM.)
  • Ba momc a cagoeli l'olo nüi cagoeli l'ognol nüi miäläb. The woman is neither good nor bad. (do-3P woman DEF.ART also DAT-good NEG.ART also DAT-bad NEG.ART 3EUM-3SM.)
◆ piireli - not

The conjunction piireli paiyeli is used with the dative case and stems from the combination of piir differ and the dative marker eli. It is used to negate nouns or entire phrases, and its syntactic properties are identical to that of cagoeli.

  • Eolin piireli äblu nälu. It was not you that I saw. (see-REDUP-1P not DAT-2SG 1SG-2SG.)
  • Piireli lis'ugu cän, nänäln stüdo ci nä. I came from the house, not from the city. (not DAT-GEN-house DEF.ART, come-REDUP-1SG GEN-city DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Dore caio piireli limibis läb. Not always does love win. (win-3P love not DAT-always 3SUM.)
  • Bal piireli ägä lidane, cagoeli libito ciäläb. You are not only stupid, but also fat. (do-2P not but DAT-stupid, also DAT-fat 2SG-3SM.)
13.3 Prepositions

Prepositions are able to attach to verbs in attributive clauses, and in those cases, they are preceded by a hyphen.

◆ pagson - from

The preposition pagson pascon is used with the genitive case and stems from pag go and the genitive marker son. It is used to mean from, since, both in temporal and spatial expressions. It can substitute the genitive case, but only when denoting origin, not possession.

  • Nänä pagson dans stü gä pa. He came from that place. (come-REDUP-3P from far GEN-place DEM 3OUM.)
  • Nüä l'äbeecuns äb'ecci gi pagson s'eligä. I haven't talked to that man since then. (NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-talk-GER DAT-man DEM from GEN-then.)
  • Orseon liräci pagson sonleüt. I used to fall from trees. (GEN-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-fall-GER from GEN-tree.)
  • dü co sonäl-pagson bans nä lit. the place of coming-from by me = the place I come from (place DEF.ART GEN-come-GER-from by 1SG)

This preposition can also be used to disambiguate phrases which use the genitive case both as an ablative and a possessive.

  • räbusca nipa a gift from him/his gift (gift GEN-3OUM)
  • räbusca spagson nipa a gift from him (gift GEN-from GEN-3OUM)
◆ nelli - to

The preposition nelli nielli is used with the dative case and stems from näl come and the dative marker li. It is used to mean to, until, both in temporal and spatial expressions. It can substitute the dative case, but not when applied to a beneficiary of an action or the location of something.

  • Üint nelli imsir a nä. I went to the moon. (come-REDUP-1P to DAT-moon DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Äbseon lidoreo orcosaio nelli l'ebaet. Maybe I'll find a partner by Sunday. (DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-win-GER GEN-partner to DAT-Sunday.)
  • Äb'ar momc lipao nelli lidü r'ecci mion. A woman can go to a man. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P woman DAT-go-GER until DAT-place GEN-man 3EUM.)
  • dü co spao-nelli bans pa the place he goes to (place DEF.ART GEN-going-GER-to by 3OUM)

This preposition can also be used to disambiguate phrases which use the dative case both as a grammatical requirement for the preceding word and in a lexical sense.

  • Sonol cagoeli liretu paion. He brings the town too. / He brings it to the town too. (bring-3P also DAT-town 3OUM-3EUM.)
  • Sonol cagoeli linelli liretu paion. He brings it to the town too. (bring-3P also DAT-to DAT-town 3OUM-3EUM.)
◆ rot - by

The preposition rot not stems directly from the word rot path and functions as the instrumental preposition. It is used with the genitive case, unless it is used to turn an adjective into an adverb (see 11.4 Conversion of adjectives into adverbs). rot is used to convey the instrumental meaning of with and by, as well as to mean through and per. It is not, however, used to mark the agent of a passive verb.

  • Eli cugus rot diipüi co paba. People see with their eyes. (see-3P person-REDUP through GEN-eye DEF.ART 3OM.)
  • Äbseosc limuu rot scatar. You can learn by losing. (DAT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-learn-GER through GEN-lose-GER.)
  • Üint rot s'ugu nä. I walked through a house. (go-REDUP-1P through GEN-house 1SG.)
  • Ar mina sonleüt rot nicus pa. There are four trees per person. (LOC.COP-3P four-NOM GEN-tree through GEN-person 3OUM.)
  • tarürü co soräbtüee-rot bans nä the money I pay with (money-REDUP DEF.ART GEN-pay-GER-through by 1SG)

The same preposition can be followed by a gerund to mean by doing if the subject is not specified, or if one does if the subject is specified. In such expressions, subordinate alignment rules apply: if the verb is intransitive, the subject is in the genitive case, and if it is transitive, the subject is preceded by the preposition bans and placed directly after the gerund, before a potential direct object, which is in the genitive form. See 18.1 Word order for details.

  • Doeseli gä rot scimisdar emimi ugu paion. By standing there one can see a house. (can.see-3P DEM by GEN-stand-GER DAT-there house 3OUM-3EUM.)
  • Äbdores rot stosuepil r'än nä. lit. By my staying, I will win. = I will win if I stay. (DAT-win-1P by GEN-stay-GER GEN-1SG 1SG.)
  • Äbseosc sair semirn rot spiisäici bans nä r'ecci co. lit. By the man's not killing by me, you may be sad. = If I don't kill the man you may be sad. (DAT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-take-GER GEN-sadness by GEN-NEG-kill-GER by 1SG GEN-man DEF.ART.)

Finally, rot can be used clause-initially before the main verb and with no additional marking to mean one should do.

  • Rot rätteciu imbi micoso ciläb. lit. By (the fact that) you tell us the truth. = You should tell us the truth. (by tell-2P DAT-1SET truth 2SG-3SUM.)
  • Rot piimadisc lipoinpiugu nä. I shouldn't work on Fridays. (by NEG-work-1P DAT-Friday 1SG.)
  • Rot mumu an cän diinop mion. The child should've learned from his parents. (by learn-REDUP-3P child DEF.ART GEN-parent 3EUM.)
◆ bans - for

The preposition bans vans is used with the direct case and originates from the verbal form bans I do. It marks the beneficiary of an action, its use sometimes overlapping with dative marking. Moreover, it is used to mark the agent of subordinate clauses (unless the verb is intransitive, excluding the copula, in which case genitive marking is used; see 18.1 Word order). Finally, it is used to mark the subject in in attributive clauses if the subject is different from the head noun (see 18.3 Attributive clauses). When marking subjects/agents in subordinate clauses, the preposition directly follows the gerund, before other arguments.

  • Peobeoms bans ciulu näpa. I did it for you (because of you). (do-REDUP-1P for 2SG 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Seon ligir bans ciulu. I was fighting for you. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-fight-GER for 2SG.)
  • Lä paiman saor speomu bans nä gä. This is a cookie made by me. (COP-3P cookie GEN-take-GER GEN-do-GER by 1SG DEM.)
  • Ninorn diii bans pa s'ugu r'än närä. I had my house chosen by him. (take-REDUP-1P choose-GER by 3OUM GEN-house GEN-1SG 1SG-3WUM.)
  • cus a somadici-bans bans nä the person that I work for (person DEF.ART GEN-work-GER-for by 1SG)
  • cus a s'elii bans nä the person that I see (person DEF.ART GEN-see-GER by 1SG)
  • cf. cus a s'elii r'än the person that sees me (person DEF.ART GEN-see-GER GEN-1SG)

The preposition bans can be followed by a gerund to mean in order to if the subject is not specified. The verb can be negated to express the meaning of in order not to.

  • Gimistarn bans gir nä. lit. I stand for fighting. = I stand in order to fight. (stand-1P for fight-GER 1SG.)
  • Caültio bans iti r'än pa. He stayed in order to kill me. (stay-REDUP-3P for end-GER GEN-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Üint emimi bans piicusoo spamt a äbmomc a nä. I went there in order not to give the flower to the woman. (go-REDUP-1P DAT-there for NEG-give-GER GEN-flower DEF.ART DAT-woman DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Piinänä l'ugu cän bans elii nicusan pa. He didn't come home so that he wouldn't see anyone. (NEG-come-REDUP-3P DAT-house DEF.ART for see-GER GEN-nobody 3OUM.)

If bans is followed by a gerund and the subject is specified, it is used to mean because. Like with rot, the subject in such clauses is preceded by bans, or a genitive marker if the verb is intransitive, excluding the copula.

  • Ar liu äb'n bans gir bans nä mion. lit. I live for fighting by me. = I live because I fight. (LOC.COP-3P life DAT-1SG for fight-GER for 1SG 3OUM.)
  • Caültio bans eb'iti bans nä r'usa pa. lit. He stayed because I would die. = He stayed so that I would die. (stay-REDUP-3P for DAT-end-GER for 1SG GEN-self 3OUM.)
  • Äbi emi bans äbsun bans nebi licusoo spamt äb'ecci. We are here because we can give a flower to a man. (LOC.COP-1SET DAT-here for DAT-LOC.COP-GER by 1SET DAT-give-GER GEN-flower DAT-man.)
  • Peobeom gä bans piäbeecasun r'än nipa papa. He did it because I didn't talk about him. (do-REDUP-3P DEM for NEG-talk-REDUP-GER GEN-1SG GEN-3OUM 3OUM-3OUM.)
◆ äbee - with

The preposition äbee avi stems from the word räbee *give and is used with the dative case. It marks accompaniment and cannot be used instrumentally as in eat with a fork. It can also be used as a general preposition connecting phrases, with the approximate meaning of with respect to.

  • Pasc äbee äbpa nä. I go with him. (go-1P with DAT-3OUM 1SG.)
  • Lälä mican rütnü soir äbee äblu. I want to eat with you. (COP-1SG nothing without eat-GER with DAT-2SG.)
  • cus a somuu-äbee bans nä the person I learn with (person DEF.ART GEN-learn-GER-with by 1SG)
  • Lä cago cus bisus äbee liräenabee pa. All people are equal in rights. (COP-3P same person all with DAT-right 3OUM.)
  • Ba olo pipa spog äbee lipipa läpa. Good means high in value. (do-3P good meaning GEN-high with DAT-value 3SUM-3OUM.)

Unlike other prepositions, äbee cannot be used attributively with nouns. Instead, the word suon suon lit. of existing (GEN-LOC.COP-GER) is used, with the following noun in the genitive case.

  • ena suon diisir a a sky with the moon (sky GEN-LOC.COP-GER GEN-moon DEF.ART)
  • liuliu suon spee nüi a life with no light (life-REDUP GEN-LOC.COP-GER GEN-light NEG.ART)

This preposition can also be used with gerunds to mean while doing, or having done if the gerund is perfect (reduplicated). The form is impersonal by default, but the subject can be specified using bans or the genitive case.

  • äbee lipeomu lit. with doing = while doing (with DAT-do-GER)
  • Raoc äbee licusoo spamt äb'n pa. He fell while giving a flower to me. (fall-REDUP-3P with DAT-give-GER GEN-flower DAT-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Sceget äbee ligir nipa pa. He died while fighting him. (die-REDUP-3P with DAT-fight-GER GEN-3OUM 3OUM.)
  • Sceget äbee ligir bans nonop r'usa pa. He died while his father was fighting. (die-REDUP-3P with DAT-fight-GER by parent-REDUP GEN-REFL 3OUM.)
  • Soisoirn äbee linänäl l'ugu cän nä. Having come to the house, I ate. (eat-REDUP-1P with DAT-come-REDUP-GER DAT-house DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Gimisdadar äbee l'i r'än pa. He stood up after I went. (stand-REDUP-3P with DAT-go-REDUP-GER GEN-1SG 3OUM.)
◆ rütnü - without

The opposite of äbee is the preposition rütnü ünü. It is used with the direct case and stems from the preposition rot by and the gerund of the negative locative copula nür. Like äbee, it cannot be used instrumentally.

  • Noen rütnü milm nä. I live without a cat. (live-1P without cat 1SG.)
  • Ebnüä lipao rütnü ciulu. I can't go without you. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-go-GER without 2SG.)
  • cus a sosoir-rütnü bans nä the person I eat without (person DEF.ART GEN-eat-GER-without by 1SG)

In nominal attributive phrases, the meaning of without is conveyed with the use of the word sünür hünür lit. of lacking (GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-GER), with the following noun in the genitive case. Note that double negation can be used to mean with some.

  • ena sünür diinid a a sky without the sun (sky GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-GER GEN-sun DEF.ART)
  • liuliu sünür scoünstiondu nüan a life with some hope (life-REDUP GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-GER GEN-hope NEG.ART)

When used before gerund, rütnü has the meaning of without doing. This form is impersonal by default, but a subject can be specified with the preposition bans or the genitive case.

  • rütnü peomu without doing (without do-GER)
  • Edest usa rütnü re bans pa scaio äb'n nä. lit. I died without loving by him of me. = I died without him loving me. (end-REDUP-1P self without give-GER by 3OUM GEN-love DAT-1SG 1SG.)
◆ insteta - during

While the dative case is used to convey the time at which something happens, the preposition insteta enscheta is used to specify a period of time over which something happens, or an event during which something happens. The word stems from a dvandva inst start + et end + the nominalizer -a (from mica thing). It is used with the direct case.

  • Insteta micugägäe, orseon lipao linalsiba. During summer, I would go to the beach. (during summer, GEN-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-go-GER DAT-beach.)
  • Seon linüüsü emi insteta paü tu. I've lived here for eight years. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-live-GER DAT-here for year eight.)
  • Ar l'äbeecuns insteta mis rearis äb'n pa. He talked for more time than me. (LOC.COP-3P DAT-talk-GER for time more DAT-1SG 3OUM.)
  • is a suon bans nä l'äbeecuns-insteta the hour I was talking for (hour DEF.ART GEN-LOC.COP-GER by 1SG DAT-talk-GER-for)
13.4 Usage of püta, coc, dataa

The word püta püta means nature, style, -ness and functions as an inanimate noun or a nominalizing affix, but it can also be used to mean like/as. In this case it is treated as a class 1 noun and the initial p is elided unless the preceding word is vowel-final. This includes inflected forms: genitive diüta jiẅta, dative m'üta müta.

This word can be used with the copula in the form of läl üta GEN-x to mean to be like x.

  • Lä püta spamt püi nipa arä. His eyes are like flowers. (COP-3P nature GEN-flower eye DAT-3OUM 3WUM.)
  • Liu püta ürmüe bans nä. You are like a brother to me. (COP-2SG nature GEN-sibling for 1SG.)

It can be used with the preposition rot, with the two words contracting into riüta iẅta, as in (peom) riüta GEN-x to mean (to do) like/as x.

  • Stagbee usa r'än riüta sostag pa. My heart is burning like fire. (burn-3P heart GEN-1SG as GEN-fire 3OUM.)
  • Madisc riüta niscir nä. I work as a fighter. (work-1P as GEN-fighter 1SG.)
  • Riüta diinid a, sobees rot nantee nä. Like the sun, I shine brightly. (as GEN-sun DEF.ART, shine-1P by bright 1SG.)

It can be used with gerunds, as in riüta GEN-(peomu) bans x to mean as if x (does).

  • Norn mirn riüta sostagbee bans nä närc. It hurts as if I'm burning. (take-1P pain as GEN-burn by 1SG 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Ba dast riüta spiicogol nipa papa. He looks as if he didn't sleep. (do-3P shape as GEN-NEG-sleep-REDUP-GER GEN-3OUM 3OUM-3OUM.)
  • Noriäciu riüta säbsun orlu l'äbeecuns äbmomomc ciulu. You act as if you can talk to women. (act-2P as GEN-DAT-LOC.COP-GER GEN-2SG DAT-talk-GER DAT-woman-REDUP 2SG.)

Finally, it can be used as part of an attributive clause, as in y diüta GEN-x y like an x. In such constructions, adjectives can be used too.

  • Micu rot somatosa diüta scä ergirolnib pa. He sings with a voice like an angel's. (sing-3P by GEN-voice GEN-nature GEN-DEM GEN-angel 3OUM.)
  • Ar surs do riüta diisir a äblu tarc. You have a head big like the moon. (LOC.COP-3P head big as GEN-moon DEF.ART DAT-2SG 3NUM.)

The word coc coc means limit and can be used in the dative case to mean as long as. The word is followed by a gerund in the genitive case, which can be inflected for tense, or negated. In the phrase following the gerund, all rules of subordinate alignment apply, though the subject can be omitted if it is the same as that of the main clause.

  • Äbseosc lidosue licoc sonämisdal (orlu) lisami soraniic. You can leave, as long as you come back before sunset. (DAT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-leave-GER DAT-limit GEN-return-GER (GEN-2SG) DAT-ass GEN-sunset.)
  • Äbmicudatas licoc sonläl resin (bans nä) nä. As long as i am young, I will continue to sing. (DAT-sing-continue-1P DAT-limit GEN-COP-GER young (by 1SG) 1SG.)
  • Piäbräb ulo ci äblu mirn licoc piinotar bans ciulu sonal a spando lärc. As long as you don't drink the dirty water, your stomach won't hurt. (NEG-DAT-give-3P stomach DEF.ART DAT-2SG pain DAT-limit NEG-drink-GER by 2SG GEN-water DEF.ART GEN-dirty 3SUM-3NUM.)

The word dataa dato means extent and can be used in the dative case to mean so much that. Grammatically, it functions the same way as coc, but always requires the subject to be specified in the subordinate clause.

  • Lä doils läl'üta gä lidataa somuoläi bans cusan scä pa. This language is so hard that no one tries to learn it. (COP-3P hard language DEM DAT-extent GEN-learn-try-GER by nobody GEN-DEM 3OUM.)
  • Lä sonib bileüt gi lidataa sebnür bans nä lisainu s'encoünt r'usa arä. This forest is so beautiful I can't express my thoughts with words. (COP-3P beautiful forest DEM DAT-extent GEN-DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-GER by 1SG DAT-express-GER GEN-thought GEN-REFL 3WUM.)
  • Ar spilp lidataa sosun s'enantee no spagpeo äbpa pa. He wasn't so weak that he couldn't walk. (COP-REDUP-3P GEN-weak DAT-extent GEN-LOC.COP-REDUP-GER GEN-ability NEG.ART GEN-walk-GER DAT-3OUM 3OUM.)

The same word can also be followed by a noun, including gerunds with no specified subject, to mean as much as. It can be used as an alternative to the negative-affirmative construction when comparing the qualities of two nouns.

  • Ar dataa sospur somadic äb'n pa. I have (as much as) a mountain of work. (LOC.COP-3P extent GEN-mountain GEN-work DAT-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Ar caian äbpa äb'n lidataa diinonop r'usa mion. I like him as much as my dad. (LOC.COP-3P fondness DAT-3OUM DAT-1SG DAT-extent GEN-parent-REDUP GEN-REFL 3EUM.)
  • Ar mican olo lidataa sosoir spisnä spee spaso pa. There is nothing as good as eating tasty meat. (LOC.COP-3P nothing good DAT-extent GEN-eat-GER GEN-meat GEN-light GEN-taste 3OUM.)
  • Lä bog lidataa r'än pa. He is as tall as me. (COP-3P tall DAT-extent GEN-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Lä spog lidataa r'än pa. He isn't as tall as me. (COP-3P GEN-tall DAT-extent GEN-1SG 3OUM.)

For adverbial phrases involving the word ca side, because these words are both adverbs and nouns, the genitive case can be applied to them as well, e.g. camon l'ugu → milm scamon l'ugu a cat on the left of a house.

  • Lä mimis scamon caabi l'el mibee, missolt pa. It is a time between day and night. (COP-3P time-REDUP GEN-left right DAT-and daytime, nighttime 3OUM.)

Finally, to use positional phrases predicatively, the locative copula is used in constructions involving nouns and adverbs, e.g. lisami s'ugu → Ar milm lisami s'ugu tarc. A cat is behind a house.   • Ormuaomuao alur ugu nüän tärion. A wolf couldn't have been next to a house. (GEN-kiss-REDUP-3P wolf house NEG.ART 3NUM-3EUM.)
  • Äb'ar store caabi äscart läb. The winner will be on the right of the loser. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P winner on.the.right DAT-loser 3SUM.)

The words sami and poinp can also be used in temporal expressions. Thus, lisami means not only behind, but also before, while lipoinp means not only in front of, but also after. If a time is specified, lisami can mean before/ago/earlier and lipoinp can mean after/in/later, depending on what follows the phrase. The first meaning applies when a genitive noun follows, the second when nothing follows, and the third when the genitive form of the demonstrative (scä) follows.

  • Adaintens lisami stoulools pa. It happened before winter. (happen-REDUP-3P DAT-butt GEN-winter 3OUM.)
  • Caült limuao l'ü iis lipoinp sonänäl r'än pa. He left ten minutes after I came. (leave-REDUP-3P DAT-minute DAT-10 1 DAT-tits GEN-come-REDUP-GER GEN-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Ninorn liuu lipaü l'el üü, gür lisami näion. I was born 25 years ago. (take-REDUP-1P bear-GER DAT-year DAT-and 20, 5 DAT-butt 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Äb'et paü gä emsaba sesirnida lipoinp pa. This year will end in two days. (DAT-end-3P year DEM DAT-2-NOM GEN-day DAT-tits 3OUM.)
  • Impica spae lipoinp scä, edet encaio läb. Three weeks later, October ended. (DAT-3-NOM GEN-week DAT-tits GEN-DEM, end-REDUP-3P October 3SUM.)
  • Edet usa lisami scä pa. He died earlier. (end-REDUP-3P self DAT-butt GEN-DEM 3OUM.)

Positional phrases using nouns or adverbs can take the genitive case instead of the dative case to express the place/time of origin.

  • Girarag ecci sosami s'ugu arän. A man attacked me from behind a house. (attack-REDUP-3P man GEN-butt GEN-house 3WUM-1SG.)
  • Nänä mata gä scamon pa. That sound came from the left. (come-REDUP-3P sound DEM GEN-left 3OUM.)

13.6 Other similar expressions

The list of true prepositions and conjunctions is rather short. As with spatial and temporal relations, words which in many languages are conjunctions and prepositions are represented with other expressions, particularly adverbs and adverbial phrases. Most commonly they come from verbs or nouns. Some of these are presented in this section. Note that in verbal phrases, both the preposition äbee and rot are valid.

◆ äbee l'adasar - depending on, according to

The verb noadars nuadars means depend and can be used with the dative case to represent both the phrase depending on and according to.

  • Äbee l'adasar limonat, äbsaesaec gä cusan papa. According to law, you can't kill people. (with DAT-depend-GER DAT-law, DAT-kill-REDUP-3P DEM nobody 3OUM-3OUM.)
  • Rot s'adasar limicoena, äb'ar cusoud a sair staipäci mion. Depending on the weather, the game may be canceled. (by GEN-depend-GER DAT-weather, DAT-LOC.COP-3P game DEF.ART DAT-take-GER GEN-cancel-GER 3EUM.)
  • Ebpiir popoup a äbee l'adasar liminspiira ci säbpepi bans si semicobbaba spisaba arä. The result will differ depending on whether we finish all tasks. (DAT-differ-3P result DEF.ART with DAT-depend-GER DAT-whether DEF.ART GEN-DAT-finish-GER by 1PL GEN-task GEN-all 3WUM.)
  • Niadar norstorere r'is äblu läläb. Our success depends on you. (depend-3P success-REDUP GEN-1PL DAT-2SG 3SM.)
  • Lä cudao s'adasar limirmicoa caio läb. Love is a game dependent on luck. (COP-3P game GEN-depend-GER DAT-luck love 3SUM.)
◆ äbee linäs - against

The verb nas nas means oppose, face and can be used with the dative case to represent the word against.

  • Dagdoe mis äbee linäs eb'is pa. Time is working against us. (function-3P time with DAT-oppose-GER DAT-1PL 3OUM.)
  • Lioüdaip coünpüi nipa rot sonäs eligä linütarür arä. His views discriminate against the poor. (discriminate-3P view GEN-3OUM by GEN-oppose-GER DAT-DEM DAT-poor 3WUM.)
  • Gir äbee linäs lisäici ermicaliuliu pa. He fights against killing animals. (fight-3P with DAT-oppose-GER DAT-kill-GER GEN-animal-REDUP 3OUM.)
  • Nans limonanat gi nä. I am against this new law. (oppose-1P DAT-law-REDUP DEM 1SG.)
  • Ar ondi sonäs l'enacoünt gä. This was a decision against logic. (COP-REDUP-3P decision GEN-oppose-GER DAT-logic DEM.)
◆ äbee l'esago - except

The verb esag esag means exclude and can be used with the direct case to represent the word except.

  • Äbee l'esago semicoena, lä milci bis pa. Except for the weather, everything is nice. (with DAT-exclude-GER GEN-weather, COP-3P nice everything 3OUM.)
  • Üi bis l'üitolä co rot s'esago r'än pa. Everyone went on the trip except for me. (go-REDUP-3P everyone DAT-trip DEF.ART by GEN-exclude-GER GEN-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Oba bis äbee l'esago s'isoü r'is papa. He did everything except help us. (do-REDUP-3P everything with DAT-exclude-GER GEN-help-GER GEN-1PL 3OUM-3OUM.)
  • Ninir esago s'odnupaim a paläb. He was excluded from the invitation. (take-REDUP-3P exclude-GER GEN-invitation DEF.ART 3OUM-3SUM.)
  • Lä cus s'esago scä sonläl mican äbpa pa. He's a person who excludes those he doesn't like. (COP-3P person GEN-exclude-GER GEN-DEM GEN-COP-GER nothing DAT-3OUM 3OUM.)
◆ äbee ligoäi - beyond

The verb goä gua means cross, go beyond and can be used with the direct case to represent the word beyond both in a spatial and metaphorical sense.

  • Äbee ligoäi sünürsosolt spoinantee r'än, elin adont an näion. Beyond my lack of strength, I see no obstacles. (with DAT-cross-GER GEN-lack-REDUP GEN-strength GEN-1SG, see-1P obstacle NEG.ART 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Rot scoäi spod go, ar bibis micaegaelt paba. Beyond that door, there is a mysterious world. (by GEN-cross-GER GEN-door DEM, LOC.COP-3P world-REDUP mysterious-REDUP 3OM.)
  • Äbseon libü semican äbee ligoäi s'elipüii scuns bans pa stäl. I can do nothing beyond watching him lie. (DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-do-GER GEN-nothing with DAT-cross-GER GEN-watch-GER GEN-say-GER by 3OUM GEN-lie.)
  • Goägoäs lopan mlic näion. I went beyond a small bridge. (cross-3P-REDUP bridge small 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Eolin spur scoäi spopütad a närc. I saw mountains beyond the window. (see-REDUP-1P mountain GEN-cross-GER GEN-window DEF.ART 1SG-3NUM.)
◆ äbee l'esbae - instead of

The verb esbae espai means swap and can be used with the direct case to represent the phrase instead of.

  • Erdiiciu äbee l'esbae r'än cipa. Choose me instead of him. (GEN-choose-2P with DAT-swap-GER GEN-1SG 2SG-3OUM.)
  • Cogol rot s'esbae sosoir nä. I slept instead of eating. (sleep-REDUP-1P by GEN-swap-GER GEN-eat-GER 1SG.)
  • Üint linoo ci äbee l'esbae nipa nä. I went to the meeting in his stead. (go-REDUP-1P DAT-meeting DEF.ART with DAT-swap-GER GEN-3OUM 1SG.)
◆ äbee lipupum - regarding

The verb pump pomp means touch, be concerned with and can be used with the direct case to represent the meaning regarding, about.

  • Äbee lipupum s'ondicago r'is, ninon norscart emi närc, coünst mins nä. Regarding our agreement, I think I found a mistake here. (with DAT-touch-GER GEN-agreement GEN-1PL, find-REDUP-1P mistake DAT-here 1SG-1NUM, think-1P thus 1SG.)
  • Dalä gä sonlä coünpüi orlu sorot spupum diitoobee diibitoobee arä? What is your opinion concerning thick thighs? (COP-3P DEM which opinion GEN-2SG GEN-by GEN-touch-GER GEN-thigh GEN-thick 3WUM?)
  • Ar agaci äbpa äbee lipupum sonäl äbee eb'is arä. He has doubts regarding coming with us. (LOC.COP-3P doubt DAT-3OUM with DAT-touch-GER GEN-come-GER with DAT-1PL 3WUM.)
  • Pump lein gän muliu miion. This book is about biology. (touch-3P book DEM biology 3EUM-3EUM.)
  • Sancasän sansciioio spupum s'üitolä r'än nääläb. I wrote a letter about my trip. (write-REDUP-1P mail-REDUP GEN-touch-GER GEN-trip GEN-1SG 1SG-3SM.)
◆ rot sponcason - except

The word poncason poncason means exception and can be used with the genitive case to represent the word except.

  • Gogon scaiagaian r'än rot sponcason scä iis mionän. My friends helped me except for one. (help-REDUP-3P friend-REDUP GEN-1SG by GEN-exception GEN-DEM one 3EM-1SG.)
  • Soirn asoir bis rot sponcason soscau närc. I eat all food except for vegetables. (eat-1P food every by GEN-exception GEN-vegetable 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Pobop siondu r'usa rot sponcason sesigeo sospur scurrio ci imbis a paion. He realized his dreams except for climbing the world's tallest mountain. (complete-REDUP-3P dream GEN-REFL by GEN-exception GEN-climb-GER GEN-mountain GEN-taller DEF.ART DAT-world DEF.ART 3OUM-3EUM.)
◆ rot spopet - as soon as

The word popet popiet means completion and can be used with a genitive gerund to represent the phrase as soon as.

  • Racintins mienarn rot spopet spaguii r'än emimi tarc. As soon as I got there, it started raining. (fall-start-REDUP-3P rain by GEN-completion GEN-reach-GER GEN-1SG DAT-there 3NUM.)
  • Äbseosc l'elii r'än rot spopet spepi somadic. You can see me as soon as you finish work. (DAT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-see-GER GEN-1SG by GEN-completion GEN-finish-GER GEN-work.)
  • Piorsadanc rot spopet säbsun bans ciulu libü cin. Call me as soon as you can. (NEG-GEN-call-2P by GEN-completion GEN-DAT-LOC.COP-GER by 2SG DAT-do-GER 2SG-1SG.)
◆ rot spam - based on

The word pam pan means base and can be used with the dative case to represent the phrase based on. To turn it into a predicate (be based on), the phrase nors pam DAT-x (lit. take base on x) is used.

  • Noreas rot spam lisiondu r'än nä. I act based on my desires. (act-1P by GEN-base DAT-desire GEN-1SG 1SG.)
  • Nir lein gän lemtol rot spam licitibemsa mirc. These books are categorized based on length. (take-3P book DEM categorize-GER by GEN-base DAT-length 3EUM-3NUM.)
  • Nir coüntolä gi pam licoüan aräion. This theory is based on conjecture. (take-3P theory DEM base DAT-conjecture 3WUM-3EUM.)

14.1 Regular verbs

Regular verbs have five forms:

  • - the infinitive, which is never used in isolation, but serves as a base for conjugation, and is also used when forming compounds; the infinitive has NSWEO-specific endings
  • - the first person form, which uses the endings -n, -s and -än, which are derived from first person pronouns nä and si
  • - the second person form, which uses the endings -ciu and -c derived from the second person singular pronoun ciulu
  • - the third person form, which is identical to the infinitive
  • - the gerund, which is used nominally and whose forms make use of theme vowels specific to NSWEO: N a, S o, W i, E u, O ü (N uses a neutral a); the gerund has NSWEO-specific endings consistent with that of the infinitive

Regular verbs do not conjugate for number. Gerunds can be derived not just from the base infinitive, but also from forms conjugated for tense, or negated forms. Base gerunds use unmarked pronouns, and reduplicated gerunds use marked ones.

First person conjugations are the most complex:

  • - the ending -s is used with verbs ending in a non-high vowel or a nasal
  • - the ending -n is used with verbs ending in a high vowel or a liquid
  • - the ending -ns is used with verbs ending in -s
  • - verbs ending in stops place an -s- before the final stop, devoicing the stop if it is voiced
  • - verbs ending in a sonorant + stop cluster place an -s- between the two consonants
  • - the ending -än is used with verbs ending in any other cluster

Second person forms are as follows:

  • - the ending -ciu is used with verbs ending in a vowel or a stop
  • - the ending -c is used with verbs ending in -s, a sonorant, or a sonorant + s cluster
  • - the ending -aciu is used with verbs ending in any other cluster

Verbs ending in -(a)ciu are always stressed on the penult. The ending -ciu triggers umlaut.

The gerund is formed in most cases by appending a theme vowel to the end of the infinitive, unless the resultant form would result in an illegal vowel cluster, in which case the gerund is the same as the infinitive. The gerund is the same as the infinitive also for verbs ending in liquids. Gerunds of verbs ending in s drop the theme vowel ü, but the ü-umlaut on the last vowel of the verb remains. To form gerunds of verbs ending in clusters, the final consonant is placed first, then the theme vowel, and finally the remaining consonant(s). These maneuvers are to ensure that the ending of the gerund is consistent with the ending of the infinitive with respect to NSWEO, since gerunds are used as nouns.

Note that theme vowels i and ü trigger umlaut.

14.2 Irregular verbs

Apart from the copulae, there are seven basic fully irregular verbs. Two of them are verbs of movement (pag go and näl come), another is the verb es put. The other four are generally no longer used in their original meaning, and are primarily used as auxiliary verbs in certain grammatical constructions, or preserved in compounds or idiomatic expressions. These include the verbs ba do, nors take, räbee give, dansc say.

Irregular verbs are characterized by shortened third person forms and an additional second person marker -l stemming from second person plural pronouns li and la. The gerunds are shortened as well, particularly in those verbs which have complex codas, in which the first syllable is discarded, e.g. nors → nosar → sar.

Although for almost all verbs, their infinitives are used when forming compounds, the gerund of the verb räbee (re) can be used for that purpose as an alternative to the infinitive. 14.4 Copulae

There is a copula and a locative copula. The copula is followed directly by its complement - a noun or an adjective. The locative copula expresses existence or location and is the equivalent of the English to be (somewhere) and there be. It is also used to indicate possession. Rather than using a transitive verb with the meaning of have, the locative copula is used, with the subject being the possessee and the possessor indicated with the dative case, e.g. Ar mis äb'n pa. lit. There is time to me. = I have time. (LOC.COP-3P time DAT-1SG 3OUM.). It is possible, however, to use the transitive verb dont hold to indicate possession.

The copulae are the only verbs to conjugate for number, at least in the first and second person. This is why SDOAUX are omitted with copulae, unless the copula is in the third person. The additional set and plural endings stem from personal pronouns. The first person set and plural endings are -bi and -(i)s, respectively, while the second person endings are -il and -al.

The forms of copulae originate from defunct verbs which are no longer in use, at least in their original meanings. The copula stems from läl *stay and the locative copula stems from seons *live. 15 Verb tenses


15.1 Overview of the tense system

The language makes use of nine tenses, although some of these would be considered aspects or moods in most languages. The tenses are formed using two strategies - applying case prefixes (genitive and dative) and applying other strategies (locative copula + gerund, and reduplication). Case marking indicates tense, with the direct case representing the present, the genitive case the past, and the dative case the future. The other strategies which interact with case marking indicate aspect/mood, the plain form representing realis, the locative copula + gerund form indicating imperfective, and reduplication suggesting irrealis.

These strategies form a 3x3 proto-matrix of tenses, which is modified in the current system due to a cyclical shift of three tenses - conditional → perfect → potential. The proto-system may be used in certain solidified expressions or constructions. For instance, the proto-system would have formed the perfect tense with a genitive prefix, and this strategy is still used to form reduplicated (modern perfect) forms of some verbs, like ba do → oba did, eli see → eoli saw. Examples can also be found is some compounds, such as irada yesterday (i- NOM + *r'ada existed), res already (*r'es put), ägä (*läläl could be + gä DEM).

15.2 Present

The present tense is used to describe all present actions, including progressive actions taking place at the time of speaking, habitual actions, and general truths. It is equivalent to both the present simple and present continuous tenses in English. There is no general strategy to indicate a present progressive actions, other than using adverbs such as misc now.

The present tense features no marking.

  • Madisc nä. I am working. (work-1P 1SG.)
  • Soirn lidüdo lisirnida libisida nä. I eat in the city every day. (eat-1P DAT-city DAT-day DAT-every 1SG.)
  • Lä leüb stag läb. Fire is hot. (COP-3P hot fire 3SUM.)
15.3 Conditional

The conditional tense is used to introduce conditions. The exact meaning is determined by the tense of the clause that follows the conditional. Present marking corresponds to English's zero conditional and the past/present mixed conditional, indicating unavoidable outcomes or present states resulting from unrealized conditions. Perfect marking corresponds to the third conditional and the present/past mixed conditional, being used for past actions/states resulting from unrealized conditions. The first and second conditionals are indicated with future marking, corresponding to hypothetical present or future actions.

  • Liubamt bamt mion, orcuso gä eb'ion nal parc. Flowers blossom if you give them water. (blossom-3P flower 3EUM, GEN-give-3P DEM DAT-3EUM water 3OUM-3NUM.)
  • Ordores nä, ar tarü äb'n pa. If I had won, I would have money now. (GEN-win-1P 1SG, LOC.COP-3P money DAT-1SG 3OUM.)
  • Piorcarst nä, mumun mican näpa. If I hadn't lost, I wouldn't have learned anything. (NEG-GEN-lose-1P 1SG, learn-REDUP-1P nothing 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Orräb'n äblu caio nüi näläb, piigiroroln nälu. If I didn't love you, I wouldn't have protected you. (GEN-give-1P DAT-2SG love NEG.ART 1SG-3SUM, NEG-protect-REDUP-1P 1SG-2SG.)
  • R'ar tarü äb'n pa, äbdonst ugu näion. If I had money, I would get a house. (GEN-LOC.COP-3P money DAT-1SG 3OUM, DAT-get-1P house 1SG-3EUM.)
  • Äbpasc liponca nä, ürnü mienarn tarc. I'll go out if it doesn't rain. (DAT-go-1P DAT-outside 1SG, GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-3P rain 3NUM.)

The conditional tense can also be used to form imperatives, if the conditional clause is not accompanied by other clauses in the sentence (see 16.2 Imperatives).

The conditional tense was marked in the old system with a dative locative copula followed by a gerund, but is now marked with a genitive prefix. Note that while it may seem more intuitive to negate conditional verbs by placing the negative affix after the genitive tense prefix, this tense, as all others, places the negative prefix before the tense marker.

  • Piormadicciu, äscitciu la. If you don't work, you will die. (NEG-GEN-work-2P, DAT-die-2P 2PL.)
  • Piorcageciu ciulu, äbräb'n äblu bü näpa. If you don't agree, I will make you. (NEG-GEN-agree-2P 2SG, DAT-give-1P DAT-2SG do-GER 1SG-3OUM.)
15.4 Future

The future tense encompasses both future perfective and imperfective actions and states. A common strategy in many languages is to use the present tense to indicate planned future events, but this is not a strategy employed in this language.

The future tense is marked with a dative prefix.

  • Äbsaec momc gän an r'usa miion. That woman will kill her child. (DAT-kill-3P woman DEM child GEN-REFL 3EUM-3EUM.)
  • Äbmadisc misada insteta is tu nä. Today, I'll be working for 8 hours. (DAT-work-1P today for hour eight 1SG.)
  • Nelli l'ieusat, äb'est nä. By July, I will have finished. (to DAT-July, DAT-end-1P 1SG.)

The future tense is also used for hypotheticals, like the word would in English. This usage is often applied in conjunction with conditional structures.

  • R'ar enantee s'iti r'usa äb'n, äbbans nä. If I could die, I would. (GEN-LOC.COP-3P ability GEN-end-GER GEN-REFL DAT-1SG, DAT-do-1P 1SG.)
  • Ägä äbpal ciulu, äbdosuepiln nä. Even if you go, I'll stay. (but DAT-go-2P 2SG, DAT-stay-1P 1SG.)
15.5 Imperfect

The imperfect tense is used to indicate progressive actions which either finished in the past or have lasted until the present. English past continuous tenses, as well as present perfect continuous correspond to this tense. When used with copulae, the imperfect tense is generally only used for states which started in the past and are still ongoing.

The imperfect tense makes use of the locative copula followed by the gerund of the lexical verb in the dative case. It directly translates to to be at doing.

  • Seon l'elii s'ugu. I was looking at a house. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-see-GER GEN-house.)
  • Ar ecci co linüüsü emimi insteta ü iis spaü pa. The man has been living here for ten years. (LOC.COP-3P man DEF.ART DAT-live-GER DAT-here for ten-NOM one GEN-year 3OUM.)
  • Nü enanteedee go cagogeedee suin äb'n s'eligä läläb. I haven't had that same strenght since then. (NEG.LOC.COP-3P strength-REDUP DEM same-REDUP DAT-LOC.COP-GER DAT-1SG GEN-then 3SM.)
  • Seosc liläl bog pagson sonläl resin orlu. You've been tall since you were young. (LOC.COP-2SG DAT-COP-GER tall from GEN-COP-GER young GEN-2SG.)
15.6 Past habitual

The past habitual tense refers to habitual actions or states taking place in the past. The habitual-progressive distinction is made only in the past, as the present tense is used for both habitual and progressive actions in the present.

This tense is marked with the locative copula preceded by a genitive prefix and followed by the gerund of the lexical verb in the dative case.

  • R'ar lisäici r'anan r'usa arä. She used to murder her children. (GEN-LOC.COP-3P DAT-kill-GER GEN-child-REDUP GEN-REFL 3WUM.)
  • Ürnüä lisoir spoagä. I didn't use to eat so much. (GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-eat-GER GEN-this.much.)
15.7 Potential

The potential tense encompasses a variety of meanings, and can be used to indicate the ability, possibility, or permission to do something. In case a more precise meaning is desired, phrases such as ar enantee speomu have the ability to do, elli rot a speomu know how to do, or ar enacago speomu have the permission to do can be used. These are also used when the sense of potentiality is combined with one of the other tenses (see 15.11 Additional combinations). The potential tense was marked in the old system using reduplication, but is now formed using the dative form of the locative copula followed by the gerund of the lexical verb in the dative case.

  • Äbseon limicuu. I can sing. (DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-sing-GER.)
  • Äbseosc liräci. You might fall. (DAT-LOC.COP-2SG DAT-fall-GER.)
  • D'äbseon lidosue? May I leave? (INT-DAT-LOC.COP-1SG DAT-leave-GER?)
  • Ebnüä lidüüs rot sorot a späbü r'än lisami scä. I can't feel the way I did before. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-feel-GER by GEN-way DEF.ART GEN-do-REDUP-GER GEN-1SG DAT-butt GEN-DEM.)

This tense can also be used in subjunctive clauses in conjunction with interrogatives (see 10.3 Potential form - any).

Negating this tense produces the meaning cannot/may not/must not, the latter meaning being the most noteworthy, since prohibition is expressed by negating the potential tense, not the necessitative tense. The use of negative articles in indefinite potential sentences produces phrases synonymous with or complementary to necessitative phrases (see also 15.10 Necessitative).

  • Äb'ar l'elii s'ugu pa. He can/may see a house. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P DAT-see-GER GEN-house 3OUM.)
  • Äb'ar l'elii s'ugu nüän pa. lit. He can/may see no house. = He does not have to see a house. (DAT-LOC.COP-3P DAT-see-GER GEN-house NEG.ART 3OUM.)
  • Ebnü l'elii s'ugu nüän pa. lit. He cannot/may not/must not see no house. = He must see some house. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-3P DAT-see-GER GEN-house NEG.ART 3OUM.)
  • Ebnü l'elii s'ugu cän pa. He cannot/may not/must not see the house. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-3P DAT-see-GER GEN-house DEF.ART 3OUM-3EUM.)
15.8 Perfect

The perfect tense corresponds to the simple past and present perfect tenses in English and marks any perfective past actions. Used with copulae, it indicates states that started in the past and ended in the past.

This tense is marked by reduplicating the verb. Note that personal endings are appended to the reduplicated forms, rather than reduplicated forms being derived from person-marked forms. Some irregular verbs have irregular perfect forms as indicated in 14.2 Irregular verbs, 14.3 Semi-irregular verbs, and 14.4 Copulae.

  • Eoli ecci ugu aräion. A man saw a house. (see-REDUP-3P man house 3WUM-3EUM.)
  • Soisoirn misan lob an näläb. I've never eaten a dog. (eat-REDUP-1P never dog NEG.ART 1SG-3SUM.)
  • Arc paim. You were nice. (COP-REDUP-2SG nice.)
  • Nünü leüt misan lipoinp s'ugu r'än arä. A tree was once in front of my house. (NEG.LOC.COP-REDUP-3P tree never DAT-tits GEN-house GEN-1SG 3WUM.)
15.9 Past potential

The past potential tense is used to indicate possibility in the past. Unlike its gnomic counterpart, it is not used to mark permission or ability, but rather events that may have happened. This tense is formed by attaching a genitive prefix to the reduplicated form of the verb.

  • Orscegitciu ciulu. You could have died. (GEN-die-REDUP-2P 2SG.)
  • R'eolin milm närc. I may have seen a cat. (GEN-see-REDUP-1P cat 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Piormadidisc eligä si. We couldn't have been working then. (NEG-GEN-work-REDUP-1P then 1PL.)
  • R'ar nipa gä. That couldn't have been him. (GEN-COP-REDUP-3P GEN-3OUM DEM.)
15.10 Necessitative

The necessitative tense describe actions that must be taken. As an alternative, or when the sense of necessity is combined with one of the other tenses, the phrases ar nier speomu have the obligation to do or ar tols speomu have the need to do are used instead (see 15.11 Additional combinations). This tense is formed by attaching a dative prefix to the reduplicated form of the verb.

  • Äbmadidic eccigi ari. Men must work. (DAT-work-REDUP-3P man-REDUP 3WM.)
  • Äb'ar gä s'inst pa. He has to be first. (DAT-COP-REDUP-3P DEM GEN-start 3OUM.)
  • Äb'arn nantee bans sun liu äb'n. I must be strong to live. (DAT-COP-REDUP-1SG strong for LOC.COP-GER GEN-life DAT-1SG.)

Care must be taken when negating this tense, as applying the negative prefix results in the meaning not have to, not must not. The latter meaning is achieved by negating the potential tense. The use of negative articles in indefinite potential sentences produces phrases synonymous with or complementary to potential phrases, though only in the sense of a lack of permission rather than a lack of ability (see also 15.7 Potential).

  • Äb'eoli ugu paion. He must see a house. (DAT-see-REDUP-3P house 3OUM-3EUM.)
  • Äb'eoli ugu nüän paion. lit. He must see no house. = He may not/must not see a house. (DAT-see-REDUP-3P house NEG.ART 3OUM-3EUM.)
  • Piäb'eoli ugu nüän paion. lit. He does not have to see no house. = He may see some house. (NEG-DAT-see-REDUP-3P house NEG.ART 3OUM-3EUM.)
  • Piäb'eoli ugu cän paion. He does not have to see the house. (NEG-DAT-see-REDUP-3P house DEF.ART 3OUM-3EUM.)
15.11 Additional combinations

Each clause allows for a maximum of one finite verb, which creates problems, particularly when it comes to the potential and necessitative tense. These tenses do not specify the time at which the action occurs, and they cannot be used in conditional clauses. To remedy this, certain nouns are used in conjunction with the locative copula and gerunds to express ability (enantee enanchi), permission (enacago enacago), obligation (nier niur) and need (tols tous). This strategy is most commonly used with the conditional tense and the perfect tense, though it can be used in the unmarked present tense as an alternative for the potential and necessitative tenses as well.

  • R'ar nier spao äb'n tarc, äbbans nä. If I have to go, I will. (GEN-LOC.COP-3P obligation GEN-go-GER DAT-1SG 3NUM, DAT-do-1P 1SG.)
  • R'ar enantee no sosoir äblu läb, äb'ar enantee no cagoeli lispagpeo äblu läb. If you can't eat, you won't be able to walk either. (GEN-LOC.COP-3P ability NEG.ART GEN-eat-GER DAT-2SG 3SUM, DAT-LOC.COP-3P ability NEG.ART also DAT-GEN-walk-GER DAT-2SG 3SUM.)
  • Neo nier stosue eb'is tarc. We had to leave. (LOC.COP-REDUP-3P obligation GEN-leave-GER DAT-1PL 3NUM.)
  • Neo enacago s'elii diibis äbpa läb. He was allowed to see anything. (LOC.COP-REDUP-3P permission GEN-see-GER GEN-everything DAT-3OUM 3SUM.)

16 Verb usage


16.1 Negation and interrogation

There are several ways to negate a sentence, based on the verb used, and the definiteness of the arguments within the sentence. One method is to use a negative pronoun, or to negate the subject/object of the sentence with a negative article. The latter strategy is applicable provided that the negated word or phrase is indefinite.

  • Soir misan pa. He never eats. (eat-3P never 3OUM.)
  • Pasc linür nä. I don't go anywhere. (go-1P DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-GER 1SG.)
  • Coünt ecci no mins arä. No man thinks that. (think-3P man NEG.ART thus 3WUM.)
  • Elin milm an närc. lit. I see no cat. = I don't see a cat. (see-1P cat NEG.ART 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Dobeencan äb'an an si. lit. We talked to no child. = We didn't talk to a child. (talk-REDUP-1P DAT-child NEG.ART 1PL.)

Non-indefinite arguments most commonly include:

  • - pronouns and gerunds, e.g. nä I, me, elii seeing
  • - words or phrases followed by a definite article or demonstrative, including defining clauses, e.g. nid a the sun, cus a s'elii r'än the person who sees me, ugu gän that house
  • - phrases whose head is a demonstrative, e.g. gä bilp the weak, gä rea more of it, gä scol r'än the fact that I'm sleeping, gä s'inst the first one
  • - words or phrases followed by possessive determiners and phrases, e.g. ugu r'än my house, scaian diinop r'än my parents' friend
  • - superlatives, e.g. cus currio a the tallest person
  • - quanitified phrases, e.g. lein boa many books, cugus sab two people
  • - phrases with ordinals or other words indicating order, e.g. räbirait ipric the third war, cus s'inst the first person, bae s'oüt the second week, the next week, paü s'odnu the last (previous) year, sirnida s'et the last (final) day
  • - phrases indicating directions or parts of something, e.g. pil the north, cadans the far side, ponca s'ugu the outside of the house
  • - unmarked class 5 abstract nouns, e.g. nib beauty, mirn sadness, pain, fear

If a sentence contains only non-indefinite arguments, or, simply put, the sentence cannot be rephrased to be negated with a negative article applied to one of its arguments, verbs are negated with the negative prefix stemming from the verb piir differ. It takes the form pii- before consonants, and pi- before vowels (p- in case of an illegal vowel sequence). This prefix precedes case prefixes used as tense markers. However, gerunds of negated verbs function as nouns, and case prefixes are therefore placed before the negative prefix, e.g. cus spielii lit. a person of not-seeing = a person who doesn't see (person GEN-NEG-see-GER).

  • Piicoln nä. I don't sleep. (NEG-sleep-1P 1SG.)
  • Piäb'eli nop a an orrä aräion. The parents won't see their child. (NEG-DAT-see-3P parents DEF.ART child GEN-3WUM 3WUM-3EUM.)
  • Piäbninie l'ugu cän pa. He doesn't have to live in the house. (NEG-DAT-live-REDUP-3P DAT-house DEF.ART 3OUM.)
  • P'eolin cusda ci närc. I didn't see the other person. (NEG-see-REDUP-1P other.person DEF.ART 1SG-3NUM.)
  • Piininon saban s'oüt siion. We didn't find the second half. (NEG-find-REDUP-1P half GEN-lead-GER 1PL-3EUM.)
  • Pielindiciu nib ciläb. You don't appreciate beauty. (NEG-appreciate-2P beauty 2SG-3SUM.)

When negated verbs are used in conjunction with negative pronouns or nouns, the latter element changes meaning from no ~ to some ~ (see 10.1 Negative article - none/some).

The locative copula is negated with the verb nür.

  • Nü tarürü co äbmomc a paba. The woman doesn't have the money. (NEG.LOC.COP-3P money-REDUP DEF.ART DAT-woman DEF.ART 3OM.)
  • Nünüä emi. I was not here. (NEG.LOC.COP-REDUP-1SG DAT-here.)
  • Nüä l'äbeecuns äblu. I wasn't talking to you. (NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-talk-GER DAT-2SG.)

The copula is negated by inflecting its complement into the genitive case regardless of its definiteness. This is because the copula stems from the verb originally meaning stay, and the genitive marking conveys the meaning of staying (away) from. When the complement is an adjective, it can be negated either with inanimate genitive marking or by using a negative article after the adjective. If the negated complement is a noun followed by an adjective, the adjective must be genitive as well.

  • Lä s'ugu sto leüt arä. A tree is not a big house. (COP-3P GEN-house GEN-big tree 3WUM.)
  • Lä niussaec a an cän mion. The child is not the killer. (COP-3P GEN-killer DEF.ART child DEF.ART 3EUM.)
  • Liu stane. You are not stupid. (COP-2SG GEN-nice.)
  • Liu dane no. You are not stupid. (COP-2SG nice NEG.ART.)

Because genitive marking following a copula negates it, any phrases following the copula which start with genitive marking, such as possessives or genitive-initial adjectival nouns, must be preceded by the impersonal pronoun gä.

  • Lä gä r'ecci co an cän mion. The child is the man's. (COP-3P DEM GEN-man DEF.ART child DEF.ART 3EUM.)
  • Lä scä r'ecci co an cän mion. The child is not the man's. (COP-3P GEN-DEM GEN-man DEF.ART child DEF.ART 3EUM.)
  • Lä r'ecci co an cän mion. The child is not the man. (COP-3P GEN-man DEF.ART child DEF.ART 3EUM.)
  • Lä gä spee nid a läb. The sun is bright. (COP-3P DEM GEN-light sun DEF.ART 3SUM.)
  • Lä scä spee nid a läb. The sun is not bright. (COP-3P GEN-DEM GEN-light sun DEF.ART 3SUM.)
  • Lä spee nid a läb. The sun is not light. (COP-3P GEN-light sun DEF.ART 3SUM.)

A nominal complement of a copula can only be negated with a negative article when double negation is applied to convey the meaning of some ~. Moreover, although adjectives can be negated either with genitive marking or with a negative article with no change in meaning, the meaning does differ between these two strategies when negative pronouns are involved.

  • Lälä r'ecci no. lit. I am not no man. = I am some man. (COP-1SG GEN-man NEG.ART.)
  • Lälä bilp misan. I am never weak. (COP-1SG weak never.)
  • Lälä spilp misan. lit. I am not weak never. = I am sometimes weak. (COP-1SG GEN-weak never.)
  • Lälä bilp an misan. lit. I am never not weak. = I am always weak. (COP-1SG weak NEG.ART never.)
  • Lälä spilp an misan. lit. I am not never not weak. = I am sometimes not weak. (COP-1SG GEN-weak NEG.ART never.)

Questions are marked with the interrogative prefix stemming from the verb darn other. It takes on the form da- before consonants and d- before vowels. The interrogative prefix always precedes tense markers, as well as the negative prefix, if one is used. This prefix is subject to umlaut. The combination of the two in the form däpii can be used as a tag.

  • Dapeomc lä cipa? What are you doing? (INT-do-2P what 2SG-3OUM?)
  • D'et madic parä? Is he finishing work? (INT-end-3P work 3OUM-3WUM?)
  • D'äbsoirn alur närc? Would I eat a wolf? (INT-DAT-eat-1P wolf 1SG-3NUM?)
  • Däliu bog? Are you tall? (INT-COP-2SG tall?)
  • Däpieli ugu cän paion? Does he not see the house? (INT-NEG-see-3P house DEF.ART 3OUM-3EUM?)
  • Lä dü milci gä, däpii? This is a nice place, isn't it? (COP-3P place nice DEM, INT-NEG?)
16.2 Imperatives

Imperatives are formed by using the conditional tense. The negative form is used for affirmative imperatives and the affirmative form is used for negative imperatives. The subject of the clause is the recipient of the imperative. If the SDOAUX is ciulu, it is omitted.

  • Ornälc emi. lit. If you come here. = Don't come here. (GEN-come-2P DAT-here.)
  • Piorsaesc spa. lit. If we don't kill him. = Let's kill him. (NEG-GEN-kill-1P 1PL-3OUM.)
  • Ürnü liuliu ololo äbpa mionon. lit. If there isn't a good life for him. = May he have a good life. (GEN-NEG.LOC.COP-3P life-REDUP good-REDUP DAT-3OUM 3EM.)
  • Piorsoirc misan lob cipa. lit. If you don't eat a dog never. = Never eat a dog. (NEG-GEN-eat-2P never dog 2SG-3SUM.)
  • Orlälä spilp misan. lit. If I'm not weak never. = May I never be weak. (GEN-COP-1SG GEN-weak never.)
  • Orbal mata nüi lapa. lit. If you make no sound. = Make some sound. (GEN-do-2P sound NEG.ART 2PL-3OUM.)

An alternative is to use the preposition rot at the beginning of a sentence, which roughly translates to should, but can also be used as a soft imperative.

  • Rot piinälc emi ciulu. You shouldn't come here. / Don't come here. (by NEG-come-2P DAT-here 2SG.)
  • Rot saesc spa. Let's kill him. (by kill-1P 1P-3OUM.)
  • Rot ar liuliu ololo äbpa mionon. May he have a good life. (by LOC.COP-3P life-REDUP good-REDUP DAT-3OUM 3EM.)
  • Rot soirc misan lob cipa. Never eat a dog. (by eat-2P never dog 2SG-3SUM.)
  • Rot lälä bilp misan. May I never be weak. (by COP-1SG weak never.)
  • Rot piibal mata nüi lapa. Make some sound. (by NEG-do-2P sound NEG.ART 2PL-3OUM.)
16.3 Passive and causative voice

Passive voice is formed with the auxiliary verb nors take followed by a gerund of the lexical verb, which acts as the direct object of the clause. Passive clauses act like other subordinate expressions, with the agent being marked with the preposition bans, and never with the genitive, since intransitive verbs cannot be passivized. Because direct objects are generally pushed to the end of the clause, the agent is typically placed between the auxiliary nors and the gerund of the lexical verb.

  • Ninir ecci säici arärä. lit. A man took killing. = A man was killed. (take-REDUP-3P man kill-GER 3WUM-3WUM.)
  • Ninir ecci säici bans momc arärä. lit. A man took killing by a woman. = A man was killed by a woman. (take-REDUP-3P man kill-GER by woman 3WUM-3WUM.)
  • Ninir ecci säici ormomc arärä. lit. A man took a woman's killing. = A man had a woman killed. (take-REDUP-3P man kill-GER GEN-woman 3WUM-3WUM.)
  • Ebnüä sair stiii bans pa s'ugu r'än. lit. I can't take choosing of my house by him. = I can't have my house chosen by him. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-1SG DAT-take-GER GEN-choose-GER by 3OUM GEN-house GEN-1SG.)

Causative voice is formed with the auxiliary verb räbee give followed by a gerund of the lexical verb, which acts as the direct object of the clause. The causee is marked with the dative case, as the recipient of the action expressed by the lexical verb. Causative expressions are used to mean either let someone do or make someone do. Since direct objects are typically found at the ends of clauses, the causee normally follows the subject or the auxiliary räbee if the subject is a pronoun, and precedes the gerund of the lexical verb.

  • Räb nal libamt liubatum tärion. lit. Water gives blossoming to flowers. = Water makes flowers blossom. (give-3P water DAT-flower blossom-GER 3NUM-3EUM.)
  • Räb momc äb'ecci diii s'ugu mirä. lit. A woman gives a house's choosing to a man. = A woman makes/lets a man choose a house. (give-3P woman DAT-man choose-GER GEN-house 3EUM-3WUM.)
  • Ren äbmomc cusoo spamt äb'ecci näläb. lit. I gave a flower's offering to a man to a woman. = I made/let a woman give a flower to a man. (give-REDUP-1P DAT-woman offer-GER GEN-flower DAT-man 1SG-3SUM.)

Passive and causative voice can be combined to create passive causative constructions. Since the head of such phrases is the passive auxiliary nors, the agent is marked with the preposition bans and follows the gerund of the causative auxiliary räbee, which acts as the direct object.

  • Nir an re bans nop a scol miläb. lit. A child takes giving by the parents of sleeping. = A child is let/made to sleep by the parents. (take-3P child give-GER by parent DEF.ART GEN-sleep-GER 3EUM-3SUM.)
  • Äbnorn re bans pa s'iti semicobbaba nipa näläb. lit. I will take giving by him of ending his task. = I will be let/made by him to end his task. (DAT-take-1P give-GER by 3OUM GEN-end-GER GEN-task GEN-3OUM 1SG-3SUM.)

The verbs nors and räbee can also be used in conjunction with nouns to create transitive-intransitive (active-passive) pairs of verbs, e.g. räbee mico make happy, heal (lit. give joy) and nors mico be healthy, happy (lit. take joy). Such verbs cannot be further passivized, but can be used in causative constructions, e.g. räbee re scaio make/let love (lit. give giving of love) and in passive causative constructions, e.g. nors re scaio be let/made to love (lit. take giving of love).

16.4 Impersonal and reflexive expressions

In every clause, the subject must be overtly stated. For impersonal sentences, the demonstrative gä can be used as a generic subject. Unlike in English, the word meaning you is not typically used in impersonal contexts. Note that intransitive sentences with gä as the subject do not require SDOAUX.

  • Lä mibee gä. It is daytime. (COP-3P daytime DEM.)
  • Eoli gä cus papa. A person was seen. / They saw a person. (see-REDUP-3P DEM person 3OUM-3OUM.)
  • Ebnü gä lidotun scä. You / One cannot take this. (DAT-NEG.LOC.COP-3P DEM DAT-take-GER GEN-DEM.)

The reflexive pronoun usa can be used as a direct or indirect object. It has no SDOAUX form, so reflexive sentences only feature the subject form in the SDOAUX. In the genitive case, it can be helpful in distinguishing possession in third person.

  • Rättäs usa nä. I teach myself. (teach-1P REFL 1SG.)
  • Eoli ugu nipa paion. He saw his (another person's) house. (see-REDUP-3P house GEN-3OUM 3OUM-3EUM.)
  • Eoli ugu r'usa paion. He saw his (own) house. (see-REDUP-3P house GEN-REFL 3OUM-3EUM.)

It can also be used after a noun to mean itself, as in something itself.

  • Edet nid a usa usa läb. The sun itself has died. (end-REDUP-3P sun DEF.ART REFL self 3SUM.)
  • Cagap oppeom cän usa paion. He heard the author herself. (end-REDUP-3P sun DEF.ART REFL self 3SUM.)
  • Dobeencan äbselit a äbsa nä. I talked to the king himself. (talk-REDUP-1P DAT-king DEF.ART DAT-REFL 1SG.)

To reinforce a personal pronoun, if the pronoun is the subject or direct object, it is repeated in the clause in the isolate form, thus existing both in the SDOAUX and the main part of the clause. However, if the personal pronoun is not the subject or direct object, the reflexive usa is used after it as a modifier.

  • Elliciu ciulu popba äbcus ebbisus gä cipa. You yourself know that better than anyone. (know-2P 2SG well DAT-person DAT-every DEM 2SG-3OUM.)
  • Dobeencan nä äbselit a nä. I myself talked to the king. (talk-REDUP-1P 1SG DAT-king DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • Üint äbpa äbsa nä. I went to him himself. (go-REDUP-1P DAT-3OUM DAT-REFL 1SG.)

The reflexive pronoun in the dative case is often used adverbially in sentences in which the subject does something to oneself or for one's own benefit, rather than for someone else. It is typically placed directly after the verb.

  • Üi äbsa l'ugu cän pa. He went to the house. (go-REDUP-3P DAT-REFL DAT-house DEF.ART 3OUM.)
  • Dononst äbsa bamt nä. I got (myself) a flower. (get-REDUP-1P DAT-REFL flower 1SG.)
16.5 Common auxiliaries

There are several words which can be suffixed on to the end of a verb to alter its meaning. Most of these are verbs, but some come from adjectives or nouns. The resulting verbs have regular conjugations, and are treated as compounds, so no umlaut is involved when they are created. The suffixed forms may differ based on whether the head verb ends with a vowel, a consonant, or a consonant cluster.

The following adjective pairs can be appended to verbs.

müi near underdo bamüi doesmüi dontüi

When using pamco/doils, the patient is the subject if the verb is transitive, otherwise, the impersonal gä is used; the agent is marked with the dative case. When nantee/bilp is used, the agent is the subject, while the patient is the direct object, just as in a regular sentence.

  • Coüntadansän dans s'ondi gi närä. I overthought that decision. (think-far-REDUP-1P far GEN-decision DEM 1SG-3WUM.)
  • Soirdansamco paiman äbcus ebbisus mion. Overeating cookies is easy for anyone. (overeat-easy-3P cookie DAT-person DAT-every 3EUM.)
  • R'ar gä lidobeenscoisal äbcugusda äb'n. Speaking to other people used to be difficult for me. (GEN-LOC.COP-3P DEM DAT-speak-difficult-GER DAT-other.person-REDUP DAT-1SG.)
  • Saecantee cugus paba. He is good at killing people. (kill-strong-3P person-REDUP 3OUM-3OM.)
  • Madicilpaciu ciulu. You are bad at working. (work-weak-2P 2SG.)

All these verbs act morphologically and syntactically the same way as if they were unmodified.

  • Soirintins ecci bamt aräion. A man started to eat flowers. (eat-start-REDUP-3P man flower 3WUM-3EUM.)
  • Äbmuaodatas eccigi neri. I will continue kissing men. (DAT-kiss-take.up-1P man-REDUP 1SG-3WM.)
  • Piir'elirbitciu cin. Stop looking at me. (NEG-GEN-see-cut-2P 2SG-1SG.)
  • Lälä mican rütnü colresinu. I want to go back to sleep. (COP-1SG nothing without sleep-new-GER.)

16.7 Valency

Depending on the number of arguments a verb takes, it can be classed as intransitive or transitive. Intransitive verbs have no direct object, e.g. col cou rest, sleep, pag pag go, dobeensc doviensc talk. Transitive verbs have a direct object, e.g. dii dai choose, gir jir fight, dont dont hold.

Verbs which can function both as transitive and intransitive are referred to as ergative, e.g. inst enst start, bae vai change, et et end. Compare Et madic parä. He finishes work., and Et madic arä. Work finishes. Syntactically, these verbs are treated as either transitive or intransitive, based on their usage in the given clause. Some polysemous verbs can be transitive in some meanings and intransitive in others, e.g. bälioü vialoü procrastinate (intr), half-ass (tr).

There are verbs which are formed with a combination of an auxiliary verb and a noun or adjective. The verb ba do is most commonly used to turn nouns into verbs, e.g. ba mata va mata sound (lit. do a sound). Such verbs are transitive - with an inherent direct object - even if they have intransitive meanings. Similarly, the auxiliaries räbee give and nors take create transitive compound verbs, despite those using nors typically having a passive meaning, e.g. räbee mirn yavi mirn hurt, harm (lit. give pain) and nors mirn nurs mirn be hurt (lit. take pain).

The valency of verbs is predominantly relevant in SDOAUX, as intransitive verbs only require subject SDOAUX (or no SDOAUX if the subject is gä or the verb is a non-third-person copula), while transitive verbs require a subject-direct object form. Apart from this, transitivity is relevant in subordinate constructions, in which the subject is specified with the genitive case for intransitive verbs and the preposition bans for transitive verbs and the copula (see 18.1 Word order).

16.8 Other expressions

There is no verb meaning want. Instead, the phrase läl mican rütnü mica lau mican ünü mica, lit. be nothing without something is used. 17 Word formation


17.1 General remarks

There are various strategies involved in the creation of vocabulary. In general, only simple terms are coined, with more complex ideas, as well as all derivational and inflectional morphology being derived from other words. These strategies include zero derivation (inst start (v), start (n)), multi-word phrases (ba dast look, lit. do a shape), affixation (cagon similar (cago same + -n DIM)), and most commonly, compouding (doespaü season (does feel + paü year)).

The language does not permit using base-form nouns attributively, such as in the English phrases tennis shoes, rice field and paper bag. A noun used to describe another noun must be either in the genitive case (e.g. asoir ermilm cat food, lit. cat's food), adjectivized (mienarn isriolt night rain, lit. nightly rain), or part of a compound (nalmirn tear, lit. sadness-water).

The primary concern regarding the formation of compounds is NSWEO preservation. Because each of the five classes has specific endings, if the head noun determines the class of the compound and its class differs from that of the dependent, its ending must be shifted to the end of the word, e.g. nib beauty + usa mind → niusab art. This is further elaborated on in 17.3 Compounds.

Derived vocabulary has a preference for using older, original meanings of words. This means that a word like *üit travel, wander which is obsolete and only used as a suppletive reduplicated form of the verb pag, although not used in its original meaning, is preserved in compounds such as süit traveler and üitolä adventure.

In general, base forms of words are used in compounds, but conjugated forms can occur as well. Here, too, there is a preference for older conjugations. For instance, the conjunction ägä but originates from the phrase *läläl gä it may be, which makes use of the old potential tense formed via reduplication. The word micobbaba task is derived from mica thing and äbbaba must do, making use of the necessitative form of the older ba *do, rather than the modern peom do. When non-conjugated verbs are used in compounds, the infinitive is preferred, but the gerund is sometimes used as well, particularly with the verb räbee (re), e.g. soreo funny (so- GEN + re give + oo laugh).

For certain classes of words, such as animals, sound symbolism is applied. The names of most animals are onomatopoeic, typically mimicking their cries, e.g. lob dog, milm cat, gägäe cicada. Onomatopoeiae are also used for words describing sounds made with your mouth, e.g. asi sigh, eti sneeze, siut suck.

17.3 Compounds

When forming compounds, the primary concern is class preservation. Each NSWEO class has specific endings and compounds must ensure that the ending of the constituent determining the class of the compound is found at its end. This is only a concern for compounds containing constituents of different classes, in which the final constituent does not determine the class. It is not relevant in compounds whose constituents agree in class, e.g. liu bear (E) + bamt flower (E) → liubamt blossom (E), or in compounds whose class is determined by the final constituent, e.g. mata sound (O) + lag dominate (S) → matalag order (S).

17.9 Umlaut

Umlaut refers to vowel mutation which occurs when an affix is attached to a word, causing a vowel (or vowels) to become closer in pronunciation to the following vowel. In the case of prefixes, the final vowel in the prefix is affected by the initial vowel of the word to which it is attached, and in the case of suffixes, the first vowel of the suffix affects the final vowel of the word to which it is attached. There are two vowels which trigger umlaut - i and ü. The former causes the preceding vowel to front and rise, while the latter additionally causes the preceding vowel to round (with the exception of low vowels).

The vowels i and ü cause the following changes. Because the language is missing a front mid rounded vowel, o rises to ü following ü. Note that i and ü do not affect each other.

The affected vowel may be a monograph (e.g. et + -i → iti), a digraph (e.g. saec + -i → säici) or a trigraph (e.g. muao + -ti → miäeti). In the case of type 3 trigraphs, only the final two vowels are affected, e.g. caio + -sin → caiesin. For umlaut to occur, there must be an intervening consonant, so a vowel directly affixed to another vowel causes no changes, e.g. rättä + -i → rättäi.

Umlaut applies in the following cases:

  • - with case prefixes, e.g. or- + müe → ürmüe
  • - with the interrogative prefix, e.g. da- + rig → därig
  • - with verbal personal and gerund suffixes, e.g. cuso + -ciu → cuseciu
  • - in compound SDOAUX, e.g. mion- + -ri → mienri
  • - with derivational affixes, e.g. en- + gir → ingir
  • - rarely in common, well-established compounds, e.g. rot + bis → retbis

Umlaut does not apply in the following cases:

  • - across word boundaries, e.g. püi + co → püi co
  • - in compounds, e.g. nal + mirn → nalmirn
  • - with verbal auxiliary suffixes, e.g. ba + müi → bamüi
  • - in adjective agreement endings, e.g. nib + uso → nib usib

18 Syntax


18.1 Word order

The word order is fairly strict. Each sentence starts with a finite verb, usually directly followed by the subject. The direct object is typically found clause-finally, immediately before the SDOAUX. The direct object can be found closer to the verb, particularly in verb phrases in which the direct object is strongly linked to the verb, like et usa die.

Other elements of the sentence, like indirect objects and adverbial phrases are normally found mid-sentence, between the subject and direct object. However, these elements may be relocated, as long as the subject is found before the direct object. Adverbial phrases can be also found at the very beginning of the sentence, before the verb. In this case, they are followed by a comma.

One of the most important structures characteristic of this language is what is referred to as subordinate alignment. This strategy is used in various subordinate phrases, whose head is a gerund rather than a finite verb. Role marking in subordinate alignment differs from that of finite clauses.

Role Regular Subordinate
Predicate Finite verb Gerund
Subject Direct case bans (transitive verbs + copula) / genitive case (intransitive verbs)
Direct object Direct case Genitive case
Other adjuncts Case marking or prepositions

Subordinate alignment is typically found in the following types of structures:

  • - attributive clauses, e.g. an sosoir ergil a child who eats fish (child GEN-eat-GER GEN-fish)
  • - interrogative clauses, e.g. Ellin cuä co speobeomu scä näpa. I know who did it. (know-1P who DEF.ART GEN-do-REDUP-GER GEN-DEM 1SG-3OUM.)
  • - tenses using the locative copula + gerund strategy, e.g. Seon licol. I was sleeping. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-sleep-GER.)
  • - passive and causative expressions, e.g. Ninir lein sacuns bans ecci mirä. A book was written by a man. (take-REDUP-3P book read-GER by man 3EUM-3WUM.)
  • - clauses preceded by prepositions bans, rot, äbee, rütnü, e.g. Äb'eliciu rot spao orlu l'ümar cipa. You'll see him if you go to a store. (DAT-see-2P by GEN-go-GER GEN-2SG DAT-store 2SG-3OUM.)
  • - clauses preceded by function nouns like coc, püta, dataa, e.g. Matägciu riüta sesciti ciulu. You're screaming as if you're dying. (scream-2P as GEN-die-GER 2SG.)

In subordinate alignment, the subject is placed directly after the verb. Unlike in regular alignment, direct objects in subordinate alignment are placed close to the verb, immediately after the subject, before other adjuncts. The exception to this is when a locative copula + gerund tense is used, in which the direct object follows the dative gerund of the lexical verb. Examples of differences between regular and subordinate alignment are presented below, using the word bans because which requires no additional marking on the following word. Because a complement of a copula can be quite long, for clarity, bans is used in subordinate alignment with the copula as well.

  • Dobee ecci äbmomc r'an arä. A man talks to a woman about a child. (talk-3P man DAT-woman GEN-child 3WUM.)
  • bans äbeecuns r'ecci äbmomc r'an because a man talks to a woman about a child (for talk-GER GEN-man DAT-woman GEN-child)
  • Cuso ecci l'ugu äbmomc bamt aräion. A man gives a flower to a woman in a house. (offer-3P man DAT-house DAT-woman flower 3WUM-3EUM.)
  • bans cusoo bans ecci sobamt l'ugu äbmomc because a man gives a flower to a woman in a house (for offer-GER by man GEN-flower DAT-house DAT-woman)
  • Lä store ecci arä. A man is a winner. (COP-3P winner man 3WUM.)
  • bans läl store bans ecci because a man is a winner (for COP-GER winner by man)
  • Ar milm lisoir spisnä tarc. A cat was eating meat. (LOC.COP-3P cat DAT-eat-GER GEN-meat 3NUM.)
  • bans sun bans milm lisoir spisnä because I was eating meat (for LOC.COP-GER by cat DAT-eat-GER GEN-meat)
18.2 Compound sentences

Sentences with multiple clauses are not extremely common. The preferred method is to use constructions with prepositions like rot by (doing), bans because (of doing), in order (to do), äbee while (doing), having (done), or rütnü without (doing) (see 13.3 Prepositions). In English these introduce new clauses to a sentence, but here, the subordinate constructions are formed with gerunds and thus kept within one clause.

However, there are certain constructions which facilitate the usage of multiple clauses per sentence. One of them is the conditional tense (see 15.3 Conditional). Another is the the potential tense used with interrogatives, used to form phrases such as whatever you do, wherever you go (see 10.3 Potential form - any).

The conjunctions ägä although, but and dar or can be used to separate clauses (see 13.2 Conjunctions). The same cannot be said about ä(b)/e(l) and, which only separates nouns or adjectives. To connect coordinating phrases, the most common strategy is to simply separate them with a comma, with no additional marking. Another is to use the word eligä meaning and, then.

  • Nänäln l'ugu cän, mamalsän usa nä. I came home and took a shower. (come-REDUP-1P DAT-home DEF.ART, wash-REDUP-1P REFL 1SG.)
  • Peobeom madidic orläb tärri, peobeom gä r'arc läpa. He (N) did her work, and she (S) did his. (do-REDUP-3P work-REDUP GEN-3SUM 3NUM-3WM, do-REDUP-3P DEM GEN-3NUM 3SUM-3OUM.)
  • Saesaec cugus ü iis paba, eligä üi l'üpolstac pa. He killed ten people, and went to prison. (kill-REDUP-3P person-REDUP 10 1 3OUM-3OM, then go-REDUP-3P DAT-prison 3OUM.)
18.3 Attributive clauses

Attributive clauses are not formed with any relative pronouns, but rather with gerunds in the genitive form, so a person who sleeps is a person of sleeping: cus scol (person GEN-sleep-GER). In such clauses, most rules of subordinate alignment are followed, such as the direct object being in the genitive case and being close to the verb rather than delegated to the end of the clause, and the other adjuncts being treated as they would be normally. Head nouns of attributive clauses are often followed by the definite article.

  • cus a s'elii sonleüt the person who sees a tree (person DEF.ART GEN-see-GER GEN-tree)
  • lein a s'ar ingoä the book that was old (book DEF.ART GEN-COP-REDUP-GER old)
  • ugu cän s'äbsar sosäici the house that will be destroyed (house DEF.ART GEN-DAT-take-GER GEN-destroy-GER)
  • ecci co s'äbeecuns ormomc l'ümar the man who talks about a woman in a store (man DEF.ART GEN-talk-GER GEN-woman DAT-store)

The major difference between attributive clauses and other constructions with subordinate alignment is the use of the preposition bans. In attributive clauses, the preposition is used when the subject of the clause differs from the head noun, regardless of the valency of the verb.

  • cus a s'elii r'än the person who sees me (person DEF.ART GEN-see-GER GEN-1SG)
  • cus a s'elii bans nä the person who I see (person DEF.ART GEN-see-GER by 1SG)
  • cus a saor s'elii bans nä the person who is seen by me (person DEF.ART GEN-take-GER GEN-see-GER by 1SG)
  • cus a saor bans nä s'elii the person I am seen by (person DEF.ART GEN-take-GER by 1SG GEN-see-GER)
  • dü co s'elii bans ecci ormomc the place where a man sees a woman (place DEF.ART GEN-talk-GER GEN-see-GER by man GEN-woman)
  • dü co s'äbeecuns bans ecci ormomc the place where a man talks about a woman (place DEF.ART GEN-talk-GER by man GEN-woman)
  • dü co s'äbeecuns bans ecci äbmomc the place where a man talks to a woman (place DEF.ART GEN-talk-GER by man DAT-woman)

In attributive clauses whose head would be preceded by a case marker or preposition in its finite clause equivalent, the preposition or case marker is attached to the gerund as a suffix and is preceded by a hyphen. If case marking is used in this context, the genitive form is always -son and the dative form is always -eli, regardless of the phonology or animacy of any arguments. If the suffix attaches to a copula, the suffix goes at the end of its complement.

  • lein a sosacuns-son bans nebi the book we write about (book DEF.ART GEN-write-GER-GEN by 1SET)
  • dü co spao-eli bans ecci co the place the man goes to (place DEF.ART GEN-go-GER-DAT by man DEF.ART)
  • düdo ci s'eolii-eli bans milm semienarn the city a cat saw rain in (city DEF.ART GEN-see-REDUP-GER-DAT by cat GEN-rain)
  • scaian cän s'iti-äbee bans nä r'usa the friend I died with (friend DEF.ART GEN-end-GER-with by 1SG GEN-self)
  • sop a sosoir-rot bans pa the hands he eats with (hand DEF.ART GEN-eat-GER-by by 3OUM)
  • ugu cän sonläl mican-rütnü bans nä the house I want (house DEF.ART GEN-COP-GER nothing-without by 1SG)

Not all such clauses require such a suffix. In attributive clauses with passive voice, the agent, which is the head of the clause, does not require the following gerund to be followed by -bans. For instance, the person that a cat is chosen by is not *cus a saor-bans bans milm stiii, but cus a saor bans milm stiii. The former sentence is still grammatically correct, but has the meaning the person that a cat is chosen for. Other clauses which do not require a suffixed gerund are those where the relation between the head noun and the phrase that defines it is general or vague, only indicating that there is a connection between the noun and the phrase, but not specifying what kind of connection it is with prepositions or case markers. Examples are presented below.

  • micottädä co sosun stupoülc the information that there was an earthquake (information-REDUP DEF.ART GEN-LOC.COP-REDUP-GER GEN-earthquake)
  • coünsamil sorsun bans nä l'elii orscaiagaian r'än a memory that I used to see my friends (memory GEN-GEN-LOC.COP-GER by 1SG DAT-see-GER GEN-friend-REDUP GEN-1SG)

Attributive clauses whose subjects are possessed by the head noun are not marked specifically for possession, as that can be generally understood from context. Thus, a phrase like a man whose wife died is expressed as a man that a wife died.

  • lein sonläl micabamt bans gatansc a book whose topic is romance (book GEN-COP-GER romance by topic)
  • ecci co s'eolii bans an sonleüt the man whose child saw a tree (man DEF.ART GEN-see-REDUP-GER by child GEN-tree)

  • ecci co somadici äbee äbcosaio the man whose partner he works with (the man who works with a partner) (man DEF.ART GEN-work-GER with DAT-partner)

Attributive clauses are also used where English would use subordinate clauses with interrogatives. Instead, generic nouns like mica thing, cus person, dü place and mis time are used as heads of attributive clauses. In this context, applying the definite article to the noun is less common, but possible.

  • Neo cusan limis sonänäl bans nä l'ugu cän pa. When I came home, no one was there. (LOC.COP-REDUP-3P nobody DAT-time GEN-come-REDUP-GER by 1SG DAT-house DEF.ART 3OUM.)

  • Rac cus a soseomu rot bog badans pa. Who flies too high, falls. (fall-3P person DEF.ART GEN-fly-GER by high too OUM.)
  • Lä s'olo mica ci speomu bans pa pa. What he's doing isn't good. (COP-3P GEN-good thing DEF.ART GEN-do-GER by 3OUM 3OUM.)

Finally, attributive clauses are used when a question is embedded in a sentence. In this case, an interrogative followed by the definite article is used as the head of the clause, typically functioning as the direct object of the sentence as well. All other rules of subordinate alignment apply, but if the subject of the question clause is the same as that of the main clause it can be omitted from the question clause.

  • Piellin lilä co sünüüsü nipa näpa. I don't know where he lives. (NEG-know-1P where DEF.ART GEN-live-GER GEN-3OUM 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Elli cusan sonlä co sonäl r'än papa. Nobody knows where I come from. (know-3P nobody whence DEF.ART GEN-come-GER GEN-1SG 3OUM-3OUM.)
  • Elin lä co s'eolii (bans nä) näpa. I understand what I've seen. (understand-1P what DEF.ART GEN-see-REDUP-GER (by 1SG) 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Elin lä co s'eolii r'än näpa. I understand what has seen me. (understand-1P what DEF.ART GEN-see-REDUP-GER GEN-1SG 1SG-3OUM.)
  • Coüntao ecci co baläns a sosancasun sonlein ligucol a aräpa. The man remembers why he wrote a book in his bedroom. (remember-3P man DEF.ART why DEF.ART GEN-write-REDUP-GER GEN-book DAT-bedroom DEF.ART 3WUM-3OUM.)

If a question clause requires a preposition or case marking, the case marking goes on the interrogative, while the preposition is attached to the gerund. If the preposition is governed by the genitive or dative case, the interrogative is marked for case.

  • Piorrätteciu äb'n äbcuä co s'äbeecasun orlu cipa. Tell me who you talked to. (NEG-GEN-tell-2P DAT-1SG DAT-who DEF.ART GEN-talk-REDUP-GER GEN-2SG 2SG-3OUM.)

When such a construction is used with yes/no questions, instead of an interrogative, the word minspiira mienspaina whether or not is used.

  • Coünst seminspiira ci s'äbpao nä. I'm thinking about whether I will go or not. (think-1P GEN-whether DEF.ART GEN-DAT-go-GER 1SG.)
  • Pielli minspiira ci sonläl bis bans pa äb'n papa. He doesn't know if I like him. (NEG-know-3P whether DEF.ART GEN-COP-GER everything by 3OUM DAT-1SG 3OUM-3OUM.)
18.4 Questions

All questions feature the interrogative prefix attached to the predicate. Questions are not marked in any way in the punctuation, although in the transliteration and romanization, a question mark is customarily used instead of a period. Questions end with a rising intonation.

Questions do not involve any other change in the word order or the structure of the senetence. With the exception of the addition of the interrogative prefix, yes/no questions are formed identically to the corresponding statements. In questions using interrogatives, the interrogative is placed in the same position that the phrase answering the same question would be placed. Compare the following statements and questions.

  • Üi l'ümar pa. He went to the store. (go-REDUP-3P DAT-store 3OUM.)
  • D'üi l'ümar pa? Did he go to the store? (INT-go-REDUP-3P DAT-store 3OUM?)
  • Madic lisirnida libisida bans sun stols starü äbpa pa. He works every day because he needs money. (work-3P DAT-day DAT-every for LOC.COP-GER GEN-need GEN-money DAT-3OUM 3OUM.)
  • Damadic lisirnida libisida baläns pa? Why does he work every day? (INT-work-3P DAT-day DAT-every why 3OUM?)
  • Äscest m'ü iis spaü lipoinp l'ugu cän nä. I will die in ten years in my house. (DAT-die-1P DAT-ten-NOM one GEN-year DAT-tits DAT-house DEF.ART 1SG.)
  • D'äscest imi lilä nä? When and where will I die? (INT-DAT-die-1P when where 1SG?)

Any question can be followed by the invariable tag däpii.

The words mins miens yes and piir pair no always agree and disagree with the proposition of the question, respectively, unlike in English, where no disagrees with affirmative questions but agrees with negative ones. This is because mins also means thus (it is the way you are saying), and piir means differ (the truth differs from your statement).

18.5 Reported speech

The word say is conveyed with the phrase läl üta, which is akin in meaning and structure to the English phrase to be like. The copula in the phrase inflects for person, and when the form ends in a vowel, an epenthetic p is added to the beginning of üta. The word is followed by a genitive prefix which marks the following direct object of the verb, if one exists. The word dansc used to be used to mean say in the proto-system, but is now defunct and only preserved in fossilized phrases like dansc täl lie (lit. say lies).

  • Lä püta semican pa. He says nothing. (COP-3P like GEN-nothing 3OUM.)
  • Arn üta stansca sosab. I said two words. (COP-REDUP-1SG like GEN-word GEN-two.)
  • Liu püta semica spaimabi mibis. You always say mean things. (COP-2SG like GEN-thing GEN-mean always.)
  • Danca äb'n täl parc. He lied to me. (say-REDUP-3P DAT-1SG lie 3OUM-3NUM.)

The phrase läl üta can also be followed by an entire sentence to mean to say that. In this case, genitive marking is not applied. When quoting someone using this phrase, the quote is told from that person's perspective, that is if a person says they did something, the pronoun "I" is used for the subject. Reported speech does not affect tenses, unlike in English.

  • Arn üta äbpasc emimi nä. I said that I would go there. ("I will go there") (COP-REDUP-1SG like DAT-go-1P DAT-there 1SG.)
  • Äblä püta obans mican näpa pa. He's going to say he did nothing. ("I did nothing") (DAT-COP-3P like do-REDUP-1P nothing 1SG-3OUM 3OUM.)
  • Äblä püta obal mican cipa pa. He's going to say I did nothing. ("You did nothing") (DAT-COP-3P like do-REDUP-2P nothing 2SG-3OUM 3OUM.)
  • Seon liläl mican rot bems rütnü läl üta räb'n äblu caio näläb. I've wanted to say "I love you" for a long time. (LOC.COP-1SG DAT-COP-GER nothing by long without COP-GER like give-1P DAT-2SG love 1SG-3SUM.)

The alternative solution is to first state the quote, follow it by a comma, and use the phrase läl üta scä to be like that. This strategy is also applied when using other verbs in this context, such as coünt mins lit. to think thus = to think that, elli mins to know that, does mins to feel that.

  • Cogoln misada nä, ar üta scä pa. He said, "I slept today." (sleep-REDUP-1P today 1SG, COP-REDUP-3P like GEN-DEM 3OUM.)
  • Lä paim mimilm tadarc, coünst mins nä. I think cats are nice. (COP-3P nice cat-REDUP 3NM, think-1P thus 1SG.)
  • Saesäicciu cipa, seon l'ellii mins. I knew that you killed him. (kill-REDUP-2P 2SG-3OUM, LOC.COP-1SG DAT-know-GER thus.)
  • Eb'itciu usa ciulu, ar coünstiondudu scä äb'n mionon. I hope that you die. (DAT-end-2P self 2SG, LOC.COP-3P hope-REDUP GEN-DEM DAT-1SG 3EM.)

19 Pragmatics


19.1 Uses of the language

Because this is a personal language that I do not expect anyone else to learn in any capacity, its primary use would be monologues. The language is not well suited for translation, lacking proper nouns and vocabulary in various areas, which I do not consider useful to myself. Even with regard to personal use, the language would be difficult to use for other people, as its vocabulary is influenced by my own beliefs and worldview. As such, it may include concepts foreign to other people, and lack other concepts which exist in the minds of others, but not in my own. Therefore, in order to use the language, the speaker must, in a way, enter my headspace and learn to look at the world from my perspective.

The language can be used for forms such as monologues, diary notes, poems, songs. Using it for fictional stories could pose issues due to its limitations, as it would be difficult to name characters since the language does not use proper nouns, but it is not impossible.

Because the language is supposed to have a pleasant sound, spoken form is encouraged, particularly in artistic contexts.

19.2 Politeness, register, greetings

There is no distinction made between formal, informal, polite or casual language. Everyone is addressed the same way. Third person pronoun use to refer to people is dictated by the speaker's judgment, not the referent's identity. This stems from my belief that a person is only worth as much as others value them. I do not believe that identity comes from within, but is a reflection of other people's perspectives on a given person. Because I fully depend on others to find meaning in anything I do, any meaning I find with regard to my own sense of self must be validated by those people.

There are no common greetings, everyday expressions used to show gratitude, apologize or show politeness. Such language use is seen as unnecessary and a mere formality void of meaning. One should not, however, shy away from complimenting others' appearance, so a greeting such as sami milcami nice ass would be perfectly acceptable. The nature of the language encourages flirtatious behavior and viewing interlocutors as objects of sexual desire. It is a language for hot people.

19.3 Personal influences on the lexicon

◆ Religion

I am not religious. I do not believe in any deities, the afterlife, or any form of the supernatural. As such, the language features no vocabulary for concepts such as god, heaven, or spirit.

◆ Death

The language distinguishes between two types of death. One is the physical, visceral, expressed by words such as saec saic kill, destroy, scet schet die, rot, death, and saecuic saiquic murder. The other is a form of death which can occur while the person is physically alive. Due to severe mental damage, usually because of a traumatic, lifechanging event, a person can change and never be the same. Their old self dies and only their physical form remains, powered by mere instincts rather than a living human being. This kind of death is related to words such as et et end, kill, et usa et usa die, kill oneself, eusat eusat death, and ecuit equit murder.

◆ Love

There are three types of love in the language. The word dango dango represents familial love, as well as trust. I believe family members are the only people who can truly be trusted. The word caio cayo represents romantic love, as well as bisexuality. There is a term encompassing both homosexuality and heterosexuality - caiosaban cayosavan - literally half-love, because either sexuality reduces the number of potential romanitc partners by half. The third type of love is platonic, also expressing friendship. It is represented with the word caian cayan - a diminutive of caio.

◆ Morality

Morality is a cultural psy-op designed to control people and prevent them from doing what they truly want. Any concepts related to moral value or judgment are absent. The words olo good and ognol bad convey the sense of high/low in value or pleasure, not virtuous and evil. Concepts such as virtue, integrity, and perversion have no place in the language.

◆ Health and happiness

Because of my history of psychosomatic symptoms, I have learned that the body and the mind are intertwined and the well-being of one hinges upon the well-being of the other, particularly in individuals such as myself. Oftentimes, I struggle to differentiate between ailments of the body and those of the mind. As such, the positive side of this spectrum is expressed using a single word mico mico which encompasses concepts such as good health, happiness, inner peace, and pleasure, while the negative is expressed with mirn mirn, encompassing illness, sadness, anxiety, and pain.

◆ Kinship terms

In many languages, kinship terms are very complex. Here, they are greatly simplified. All kinship terms are gender-neutral, but the adjectives ecci etchi male and momc monc female can be used to specify gender, e.g. müe müe sibling → müe ecci müe etchi brother, müe momc müe monc sister.

Only closest family members have their unique terms, including müe müe sibling, nop nop parents, an an child, nopingoä nopiengua grandparents, anresin aniehien grandchild. Any other family members are derived from the word for the close family member of the same generation and the word saban half, thus sabanmüe savammüe is used for any family members of the same generation as the speaker other than their immediate siblings, such as cousins, step-siblings, siblings-in-law, etc. The word nosabaip nosavaip is used for family members one generation older than the speaker, and so on.

Some words for family members are class two nouns. For instance, nop by default refers to both parents, and is reduplicated when referring to only one of them. The term nopingoä refers to grandparents, and is unmarked when either referring to one pair of grandparents, or all four.

◆ Directions

Because I often mistake east with west, I put west on the right side of the compass and east on the left. This means that south is at the top and north at the bottom. Relative directions (right, left, up, down) are not differentiated from cardinal ones. The word rio yo means both south and up, and the same is true for the other three.

◆ Colors

There are no specific adjectives for colors. Instead, the word noba nova color is used, followed by a genitive noun, e.g. noba sonal blue (color of water), noba spüta green (color of nautre). These nouns are not set, and the speaker is free to use any object as a color reference.
